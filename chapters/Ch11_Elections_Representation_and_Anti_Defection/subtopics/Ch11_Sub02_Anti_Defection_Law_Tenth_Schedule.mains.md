Key Legal Provisions Regulating Political Funding

#### A. Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1951
1. Section 29B: Entitles political parties to accept voluntary contributions from any person or company
(excluding foreign sources and government companies).
2. Section 29C: Mandates parties to report all donations exceeding ₹20,000 to the Election Commission
(ECI) annually. If they fail to report, they lose tax exemptions.
3. Section 77: Requires every candidate to keep a separate and correct account of all expenditure incurred
between the date of nomination and the date of declaration of result.

#### B. Income Tax Act, 1961
1. Section 13A: Grants 100% tax exemption to political parties on their income (from house property,
voluntary contributions, capital gains) provided they:
a. Maintain detailed books of account.
b. Record the name and address of every person who contributes more than ₹20,000.
c. Do not accept cash donations exceeding ₹2,000.

#### C. Companies Act, 2013
1. Section 182: Regulates corporate funding.
a. Pre-2017: Companies could only donate up to 7.5% of their average net profits.
b. Post-2017: The 7.5% cap was removed, allowing unlimited corporate funding.
2. Any donation must be authorized by a resolution passed by the Board of Directors.

#### D. Foreign Contribution Regulation Act (FCRA), 2010
1. Political parties and candidates are strictly prohibited from accepting foreign contributions.
2. 2016/2018 Retroactive Amendment: The definition of "foreign source" was changed to exempt
Indian-owned subsidiaries of foreign companies.
In Association for Democratic Reforms & Anr. v. Union of India & Ors Case, a 5-judge bench struck down the
Electoral Bond Scheme as unconstitutional (). The court ruled that:
- It violated the Voter's Right to Information under Article 19(1)(a).
- The Anonymity provided by the bonds allowed for secret corruption and "crony capitalism."
- Amendments to the Companies Act (allowing unlimited funding) and the RPA (exempting bond
reporting) were also declared void.

One Nation One Election
ONOE refers to the proposal to synchronize the timing of all elections in India to occur within a single
timeframe or cycle.
History of ONOE
1. Simultaneous elections were the norm in India for the first four general election cycles (1952, 1957, 1962,
and 1967).
2. The cycle was broken due to the premature dissolution of several State Assemblies in 1968 and 1969,
followed by the dissolution of the Lok Sabha in 1970 and fresh polls in 1971.
3. The Law Commission (170th Report, 1999) and the Parliamentary Standing Committee (2015) strongly
advocated for returning to simultaneous polls to ensure governance stability.
Key Benefits of One Nation One Election
1. Reduced Financial Burden for the exchequer - costs of logistics, polling staff, and security.
2. Continuity in Governance: Prevents "policy paralysis" caused by the frequent imposition of the Model
Code of Conduct (MCC).

3. Reduced Administrative Strain: Teachers and police officers can focus on their primary duties rather
than frequent election duty.
4. Higher Voter Turnout: Consolidating elections reduces "voter fatigue" and encourages citizens to vote
for all tiers at once.
5. Curbing Black Money: Limits the opportunities for political parties to raise and spend unaccounted cash
in multiple cycles.
6. Focus on Long-term Policy: Parties can focus on nation-building for five years rather than staying in
"perpetual campaign mode."
7. Social Cohesion: Reduces frequency of communal or caste-based mobilization during election periods.
8. Supply Chain Stability: Minimizes disruptions to businesses and production cycles caused by frequent
holidays and rallies.
9. Predictable Investment Climate: Stability in governance timelines provides certainty to global
investors regarding policy consistency.
10. Efficiency for Parties: Smaller parties save on repetitive campaigning, allowing a more level playing
field against wealthy national parties.
Major Challenges in One Nation One Election
1. Constitutional Amendments: Requires amending Articles 83, 85, 172, and 174, which require political
consensus.
2. Impact on Federalism: Critics argue it undermines state autonomy by making regional polls subservient
to the national narrative.
3. Voter Confusion: Simultaneous polls might lead to voters mixing up local, state, and national issues.
4. A state might face frequent mid-term polls as, if a government falls mid-term, the replacement would
only serve the "unexpired term.
5. Logistical issue: ECI would need nearly double the current inventory of machines to support 1 million+
booths.
6. Regional Issues Sidelined: National security or foreign policy can overshadow local issues like water or
electricity.
7. Accountability Deficit: Voters only get to "punish" or "reward" politicians once in five years rather than
more frequently.
8. Security Deployment: Providing high-level security for all booths across India simultaneously is a
massive paramilitary challenge.
9. Judicial Workload: A surge in election petitions all at once could overwhelm the High Courts.
Key Provisions of the ONOE Bill
1. Article 82A (Appointed Date): The President will notify an "Appointed Date" (expected after 2024 polls)
to trigger the synchronization.
2. Article 83 & 172 Amendments: Stipulates that if the Lok Sabha or Assembly is dissolved prematurely, the
new House serves only the unexpired term.
3. Phased Implementation:
a. Phase 1: Simultaneous elections for Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.
b. Phase 2: Local body (Panchayat/Municipality) elections to be held within 100 days of the general
polls.
4. Common Electoral Roll: Amendment to Article 325 to allow ECI to prepare a single roll for all three tiers
of government.
Way Forward
1. Building Consensus: Engage with regional parties through the Joint Parliamentary Committee to
address federalism concerns.
2. Scale up the production of M3-version EVMs to meet the massive simultaneous demand.
3. Explore the German "Constructive Vote of No Confidence" model to prevent premature dissolutions.
4. Voter Education: nationwide awareness drive to help voters distinguish between local and national
issues.
