Constitution
The Constitution serves as the foundational law on which other laws are constructed and enforced.
A Constitution is a set of fundamental legal-political rules that:
1. Bind all state actors and institutions, including the legislature, executive, and judiciary, ensuring
supremacy over ordinary laws.
2. Define the framework of government by laying down its structure, functions, operating procedures,
political principles, and the rights and duties of citizens.
3. Delineate the distribution of power in society and delegates specific decision-making authority to
various entities or authorities. Eg. 7th Schedule
4. Set forth the fundamental principles and objectives that govern the establishment and governance of a
state. Eg. Preamble and the Directive Principles of State Policy.
5. Derive legitimacy from public acceptance, reflecting broad consensus and societal trust.

Indian Constitution is often criticized as a Bag of Borrowings, having its features picked up from various
other Constitutions.
Arguments in favor
1. Fundamental Rights borrowed from the United States Constitution.
2. Directive Principles of State Policy is inspired by the Irish Constitution
3. The Parliamentary System including the bicameral legislature and the role of the Prime Minister,
mirrors the British model.
4. Federal Structure with a strong central government was influenced by the Canadian Constitution.
5. Emergency Provisions were derived from the Weimar Constitution of Germany.
6. Fundamental Duties was inspired by the Soviet Constitution.
7. The concurrent List was borrowed from the Australian Constitution.
8. Judicial Review was adopted from the American system
Arguments Against
'Nobody holds any patent rights in fundamental ideas of the Constitution' - Ambedkar.
1. The crafting and implementation of its provisions were guided and adapted by Indians to accommodate
the diverse local realities. Eg. Federalism.
2. Indian civilizational values: Equality, fraternity, liberty, federalism, republic, democracy, etc. can be
traced back to ancient Indian history and scriptures. For Eg.
a. The secularism in the Indian Constitution is influenced by 'sarva dharma sambhav'
b. Equality principles under Article 14 to 18 are inspired from Rig Veda that emphasizes the idea of the
unity of all beings and the absence of discrimination based on caste or birth.
c. The Arthashastra calls individual liberties and property rights as essential components of good
governance
d. The establishment of the "Mahasangha" or the Great Assembly in ancient India is considered an
early form of a republic.
e. The concept of "Sabha" and "Samiti" in ancient texts reflects democratic practices.
3. Unique Synthesis, Not Blind Copying - Eg. Fundamental Rights inspired by the US Bill of Rights, but
subject to reasonable restrictions reflecting Indian socio-economic realities.
4. Certain basic features are common in all the democratic constitutions in the world. Eg. Protection of
individual rights, separation of powers, rule of law.
5. Many provisions were of Indian origin. Eg. Finance Commission, Reservation, etc.
Constitution as a living document
According to Woodrow Wilson, living political Constitutions must be Darwinian in structure and in practice.
1. Balance between rigidity and flexibility under Article 368.
2. Judicial Interpretation - Eg. Right to Privacy (2017) recognized as part of Article 21; Basic Structure
Doctrine (1973) ensures continuity with adaptability.
3. Expansion of Fundamental Rights - Eg. Right to Education (2002) made a Fundamental Right (Art.
21A); environmental protection interpreted within Right to Life.

Limitations and Challenges
1. Overuse of Amendments - Excessive amendments (100+ so far) risk diluting constitutional sanctity. Eg.
Frequent changes to Reservation policy (77th, 81st, 85th, 103rd Amendments)
2. Judicial Overreach Concerns (against separation of powers) - Eg. NJAC Judgment.
3. Ambiguity in "Basic Structure Doctrine" - the doctrine is vague and judges decide its scope case by
case, leading to uncertainty.
4. Tension Between Flexibility and Stability - Eg. Emergency provisions (1975-77 misuse) showed how
flexibility can weaken rights.
5. Socio-economic Gaps in Realization - Eg. Directive Principles like right to work or nutrition remain
partially realized despite MGNREGA and NFSA.
6. Political Motives - Eg. 42nd Amendment (1976) tried to concentrate power in the centre.
7. Complexity for Common Citizens - The Constitution's vast length and legal language make it less
accessible to ordinary people, challenging its participatory legitimacy.
Constitutionalism
Constitutionalism is a political philosophy centered on limiting government power through a Constitution,
ensuring government operates within the bounds of law and protecting fundamental rights.
In the I. R. Coelho case, the Supreme Court held that "Constitutionalism is a legal principle that requires
control over the exercise of Governmental power to ensure that it does not destroy the democratic principles
upon which it is based".
In the Rameshwar Prasad case, the Supreme Court held that "the constitutionalism or constitutional system
of Government abhors absolutism - it is premised on the Rule of Law in which subjective satisfaction is
substituted by objectivity provided by the provisions of the Constitution itself".
Louis Henkin defines Constitutionalism as constituting the following 9 elements:
1. Government according to the Constitution - it provides for codification of fundamental laws and
principles into a single, formal document that serves as the supreme law of the land.
2. Separation of power - it prevents the concentration of power, ensures checks and balances, and
safeguards against potential abuses. Eg. impeachment process
3. Sovereignty of the People - Representative institutions derive power from popular mandate, upholding
the principle of government by the people, for the people, of the people.
4. Constitutional Review - Judiciary ensures supremacy of the Constitution;
a. Raja Ram Pal case (2007): Judicial review extends to parliamentary proceedings if they violate
constitutional principles.
b. I.R. Coelho case (2007): Laws placed in Ninth Schedule after 1973 subject to Basic Structure
Doctrine, reinforcing constitutional supremacy.
5. Independent judiciary - to ensure that laws are applied fairly, protecting individual rights and upholding
the rule of law without external influence.

The Preamble is the introductory statement that outlines the philosophy, ideals, and objectives of the
Constitution.
It reflects the vision of the Constituent Assembly and acts as the identity card of the Constitution.
We, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST
SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:
JUSTICE, Social, Economic and Political.
LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
EQUALITY of status and of opportunity and to promote among them all.
FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation.
In Our Constituent Assembly, this 26th day of November 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT, and GIVE TO
OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.
Significance of the Preamble
1. Philosophical Foundation - Serves as the soul of the Constitution, reflecting ideals of justice, liberty,
equality, fraternity.
2. Source of Authority - Declares that the power rests with the people, establishing popular sovereignty.
3. Guiding Principles - Acts as a compass for governance and guides constitutional interpretation.
4. Identity of the State - Defines India as Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic.
5. Vision of Social Revolution - Highlights the goal of transforming Indian society based on social,
economic, and political justice.
6. Inspirational Value - Inspires citizens and institutions to uphold constitutional morality and
democratic values.
7. Judicial Reference - Frequently used by the Supreme Court to uphold the basic structure. Eg.
Kesavananda Bharati, 1973.
8. National Unity - Reinforces unity in diversity through the ideals of fraternity and integrity.

Scholars
1. In Sajjan Singh vs. State of Rajasthan, Justice Madholkar said that the Preamble had the stamp of "deep
deliberation" and "an epitome" of the broad features of the Constitution.
2. Sir Ernest Barker's Tribute: called the Preamble the "keynote" of the Constitution and quoted it in his
book Principles of Social and Political Theory (1951).
3. N.A. Palkhivala - the "horoscope of the Constitution".
Criticism of the Preamble
1. Non-justiciable nature - Ideals in the Preamble are not legally enforceable, reducing their practical
impact.
2. Redundancy argument - Critics argue it merely restates principles already covered in Fundamental
Rights and DPSPs.
3. Ambiguity - Terms such as justice, equality, fraternity are abstract and open to subjective
interpretation.
4. Borrowed Principles - Critics note inspiration from the US and French constitutions, questioning
originality
5. Symbolic over substantive - Seen as more of a moral declaration than a practical instrument of
governance.
6. Risk of Judicial Overreach - Its use in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and later cases gives courts wide
discretion, raising fears of judicial legislation.
Debate surrounding whether preamble is part of constitution or not
Judicial Debate on the Preamble
1. Berubari Case (1960) - SC held that the Preamble is not part of the Constitution. It serves only as a guide
to understand the farmers' intentions. Not enforceable in a court of law.
2. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) - described Preamble as the "very soul" of the Constitution, eternal
and unalterable. Though not enforceable, it represents the core principles guiding government.
3. Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973)
a. SC overruled Berubari; held the Preamble is part of the Constitution.
b. Declared the Preamble as part of the Basic Structure.
c. It can be amended, but only if the Basic Structure is not altered.
d. Established its role as an interpretative tool for constitutional provisions.
4. LIC of India v. Union of India (1995) - Reaffirmed that the Preamble is an integral part of the
Constitution.
5. Present Position -Preamble is part of the Constitution. It is non-justiciable, but has interpretative value.
Embodies the Basic Structure, restricting Parliament's amending powers.
Salient Features and keywords in Preamble
Socialism
It is a governmental system that advocates community ownership and control of all lands and businesses
rather than individual ownership. The word 'Socialist', added in the Preamble by 42nd Amendment Act, 1976
aims to eliminate inequality in income, status and standard of living.
