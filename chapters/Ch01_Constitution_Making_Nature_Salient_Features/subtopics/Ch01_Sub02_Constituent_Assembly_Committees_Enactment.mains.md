Preamble Of The Indian Constitution

### PYQs

- **[2019]** Do you think that the Constitution of India does not accept the principle of strict separation of
powers rather it is based on the principle of 'checks and balances'? Explain. (10)
The Preamble is the introductory statement that outlines the philosophy, ideals, and objectives of the
Constitution.
It reflects the vision of the Constituent Assembly and acts as the identity card of the Constitution.
We, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST
SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:
JUSTICE, Social, Economic and Political.
LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
EQUALITY of status and of opportunity and to promote among them all.
FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation.
In Our Constituent Assembly, this 26th day of November 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT, and GIVE TO
OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.
Significance of the Preamble
1. Philosophical Foundation - Serves as the soul of the Constitution, reflecting ideals of justice, liberty,
equality, fraternity.
2. Source of Authority - Declares that the power rests with the people, establishing popular sovereignty.
3. Guiding Principles - Acts as a compass for governance and guides constitutional interpretation.
4. Identity of the State - Defines India as Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic.
5. Vision of Social Revolution - Highlights the goal of transforming Indian society based on social,
economic, and political justice.
6. Inspirational Value - Inspires citizens and institutions to uphold constitutional morality and
democratic values.
7. Judicial Reference - Frequently used by the Supreme Court to uphold the basic structure. Eg.
Kesavananda Bharati, 1973.
8. National Unity - Reinforces unity in diversity through the ideals of fraternity and integrity.

Scholars
1. In Sajjan Singh vs. State of Rajasthan, Justice Madholkar said that the Preamble had the stamp of "deep
deliberation" and "an epitome" of the broad features of the Constitution.
2. Sir Ernest Barker's Tribute: called the Preamble the "keynote" of the Constitution and quoted it in his
book Principles of Social and Political Theory (1951).
3. N.A. Palkhivala - the "horoscope of the Constitution".
Criticism of the Preamble
1. Non-justiciable nature - Ideals in the Preamble are not legally enforceable, reducing their practical
impact.
2. Redundancy argument - Critics argue it merely restates principles already covered in Fundamental
Rights and DPSPs.
3. Ambiguity - Terms such as justice, equality, fraternity are abstract and open to subjective
interpretation.
4. Borrowed Principles - Critics note inspiration from the US and French constitutions, questioning
originality
5. Symbolic over substantive - Seen as more of a moral declaration than a practical instrument of
governance.
6. Risk of Judicial Overreach - Its use in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and later cases gives courts wide
discretion, raising fears of judicial legislation.
Debate surrounding whether preamble is part of constitution or not
Judicial Debate on the Preamble
1. Berubari Case (1960) - SC held that the Preamble is not part of the Constitution. It serves only as a guide
to understand the farmers' intentions. Not enforceable in a court of law.
2. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) - described Preamble as the "very soul" of the Constitution, eternal
and unalterable. Though not enforceable, it represents the core principles guiding government.
3. Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973)
a. SC overruled Berubari; held the Preamble is part of the Constitution.
b. Declared the Preamble as part of the Basic Structure.
c. It can be amended, but only if the Basic Structure is not altered.
d. Established its role as an interpretative tool for constitutional provisions.
4. LIC of India v. Union of India (1995) - Reaffirmed that the Preamble is an integral part of the
Constitution.
5. Present Position -Preamble is part of the Constitution. It is non-justiciable, but has interpretative value.
Embodies the Basic Structure, restricting Parliament's amending powers.
Salient Features and keywords in Preamble
Socialism
It is a governmental system that advocates community ownership and control of all lands and businesses
rather than individual ownership. The word 'Socialist', added in the Preamble by 42nd Amendment Act, 1976
aims to eliminate inequality in income, status and standard of living.

Indian Socialism
1. 42nd Amendment, 1976: The term "Socialist" was added, but the Directive Principles of State Policy
(DPSPs) already reflected socialist ideals.
2. Nehruvian Socialism: state-led industrialization, central planning, and public sector control of key
industries.
3. Five-Year Plans: promoted industrialization through significant state intervention.
4. Public Sector Dominance: The government controlled key industries like steel and mining through
public enterprises.
5. Land Reforms: Socialist principles drove land distribution efforts to address rural inequality.
6. Gandhian Socialism -
a. For Gandhi, socialism meant equality, where all-prince, peasant, wealthy, poor, employer, and
employee-are equal.
b. Article 40: Promotes village panchayats to handle local administration, in line with Gandhian ideals.
c. Article 43: Encourages the promotion of cottage industries in rural areas. Eg. Khadi and Village
Industries Commission (KVIC).
d. Article 43(B): Supports the voluntary formation and democratic control of cooperative societies (97th
CAA, 2011).
e. Social Justice and Welfare: Articles 15, 16, and 46 reflecting Gandhian Sarvodaya ideals by preventing
discrimination and supporting marginalized sections.
Challenges to socialism in India
Political
1. Populism over Principle: Political class often pursues short-term welfare schemes for electoral gains,
weakening socialist vision. Eg. loan waiver schemes
2. Poor Land Reform Implementation: By 2024, land reforms redistributed only 4% of India's total
agricultural land.
Economic
1. LPG Reforms (1991 onwards): Shift from socialist planning to market-oriented policies (liberalisation,
privatisation, globalisation).
2. Global Integration: Emphasis on foreign investment, trade competitiveness, and market efficiency
over state-led redistribution.
3. Wealth Inequality: As per Oxfam report, the top 1% in India held over 40.5% of the country's total
wealth, exacerbating inequality.
4. Privatization Trends: Eg. Strategic disinvestment in PSUs
5. Globalization Pressures: WTO obligations limited India's agricultural subsidies under the Agreement on
Agriculture, restricting state control.
Socio-Cultural
1. Middle-Class Aspirations: Growing focus on material prosperity and consumerism clashes with
collective welfare ideals.
2. Individualism vs. Collectivism: Aspirations for personal success increasingly overshadow socialist
emphasis on equality and fraternity.
Environmental
1. Unsustainable development - Over-exploitation of resources disproportionately harms the poor,
eroding Gandhian socialist justice.

- Rajni Kothari - Warned that coalition politics and rise of regional parties dilute ideological
commitments, reducing socialism to populist slogans.
- Amartya Sen - Highlighted that social opportunity failures (health, education, gender justice) persist
despite growth, showing India's socialism failed to deliver capabilities-based equality.
- Prabhat Patnaik - Argued that globalisation and neoliberal reforms hollow out socialist policies by
prioritising capital flows over welfare.
- Granville Austin - Pointed out that India's Constitution envisions a "social revolution", but political
practice has lagged in translating socialism into social justice.
SC judgments on Socialism
1. D.S. Nakara v. Union of India (1983) - Defined Indian Socialism as providing "a decent standard of life"
and "security from cradle to grave."
2. AK Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) - Initially restricted individual liberty in favor of state-led social
goals before the "Due Process" era.
3. Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): Recognized livelihood as part of the right to life
under Article 21.
4. Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): Upheld the right to dignity and fair wages as
Fundamental Rights under Article 21.
5. D.S. Nakara v. Union of India (1983) - Expanded right to pension as a measure of socio-economic
justice, calling India a welfare state rooted in socialism.
6. Sanjeev Coke Mfg. Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. (1983) - Upheld nationalisation of coal mines,
affirming socialism as part of constitutional philosophy.
7. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) - Held that harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs is
essential. Socialism under Preamble guides this balance.
8. Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997) - Barred transfer of tribal land to non-tribals, emphasising
socialist commitment to protect weaker sections.
Way Forward for Socialism in India
1. Reduce Inequality through Progressive Welfare - Strengthen progressive taxation and expand Direct
Benefit Transfer (DBT) schemes. Eg. PM-KISAN, MGNREGA.
2. Universal Education and Healthcare - increasing spending on Health and Education to 2.5% and 6% of
GDP respectively.
3. Revitalise Public Sector & Cooperative Models - Eg. Amul cooperative model.
4. Empower Marginalised Communities - Strengthen affirmative action, and tribal welfare
programmes.
5. Sustainable & Equitable Development - Adopt green growth strategies that balance industrialisation
with environmental justice. Eg. Green India mission
Secularism
Secularism is a principle or ideology that advocates for the separation of religious institutions and beliefs
from the affairs of the state, government, or public institutions.
SC in the Subramanian Swamy case, upheld the inclusion of 'socialist, secular' in the Preamble of the
Constitution.

SC in the S.R. Bommai (1994) declared secularism a basic structure.
According to Akeel Bilgrami, Secularism consists of three commitments
1. Freedom of religious belief and practice,
2. Principles enshrined in the Constitution such as equality, freedom of speech, gender equality
3. Meta-commitment, which says if there is a clash between the first and second commitments, the
second commitment must get priority
Secularism under the Indian constitution
1. Article 15: Prohibits discrimination based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, ensuring equal
treatment by the state.
2. Article 16: Guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment, prohibiting discrimination on
various grounds.
3. Articles 25-28: Ensures freedom of religion, allowing individuals to practice and propagate their religion,
subject to public order.
4. Article 28: Protects the rights of minorities to establish and manage educational institutions.
5. Articles 29 & 30: Provide cultural and educational rights to protect minority interests.
6. Article 44: Promotes the idea of a Uniform Civil Code to establish common laws for all citizens.
7. Article 51A: Fundamental duty to promote harmony, preserve cultural heritage, and foster brotherhood.
Significance of secularism
Political Significance
1. Protection of Individual Rights - Eg. Article 25 guarantees freedom of religion.
2. Prevention of Religious Discrimination - Separation of state and religion discourages imposition of
beliefs.
3. Promotion of Rational Governance - Policies based on rationality, not religion. Eg. Uniform civil code.
4. Protects Minority Rights - Eg. Article 30 ensures minorities' right to establish institutions.
5. Strengthens Democracy - Prevents fusion of state and religion. equal citizenship for all. (Rajeev
Bhargava - principled distance).
6. Supports Constitutional Morality - Upholds justice, liberty, equality, fraternity.
7. Encourages Global Standing - Projects India as a tolerant and inclusive democracy.
Social Significance
1. Preserves Unity in Diversity - Ensures coexistence of multiple religions, castes, and cultures.
2. Promotes Social Justice - Reforms regressive practices. Protects gender and caste equality. Eg. Shayara
Bano (2017) struck down instant triple talaq.
3. Prevents Communal Conflicts - Safeguards against polarisation and religious violence.
