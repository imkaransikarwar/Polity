| Veto over legislation | **1.** The President has an absolute, a suspensive and a pocket veto under Art 111, which prescribes no time limit for assent.<br>**2.** If a Bill is returned and passed again, he must assent.<br>**3.** He has no veto over a Constitutional Amendment Bill since the 24th Amendment, 1971. | **1.** The President may veto a Bill within ten days.<br>**2.** Congress may override the veto by two thirds of both Houses.<br>**3.** A pocket veto operates only where Congress adjourns within those ten days. | India has no legislative override of a Presidential veto. The Indian remedy is repassage, which then binds the President. |
| Judicial review and the rights standard | **1.** Implicit, resting on Arts 13, 32 and 226, and part of the Basic Structure.<br>**2.** Art 21 uses procedure established by law, taken from Japan, though Maneka Gandhi, 1978 read fairness into it. | **1.** Not expressed in the Constitution; established by Marbury v. Madison, 1803.<br>**2.** The Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments use due process of law, a wider standard. | India borrowed judicial review and the Fundamental Rights from the USA but chose the narrower procedural standard in Art 21. |

| Amending the Constitution | **1.** Art 368: special majority of each House, and for the entries in the proviso, ratification by half the States by simple majority.<br>**2.** The Basic Structure doctrine limits the amending power. | **1.** Article V: proposal by two thirds of both Houses or by a convention, then ratification by three fourths of the States, that is 38 of 50.<br>**2.** There is no Basic Structure doctrine. | India's is a blend of rigidity and flexibility; some provisions need only a simple majority. The American Constitution is uniformly rigid, which is why it has only 27 amendments. |
| Nature of the federation and citizenship | **1.** Art 1: a Union of States, not the product of an agreement, and no State may secede.<br>**2.** Single citizenship, a single integrated judiciary, and residuary powers with the Union. | **1.** A federation formed by agreement among the States, described as an indestructible union of indestructible States.<br>**2.** Dual citizenship, a dual judiciary, and residuary powers with the States under the Tenth Amendment. | Ambedkar: India is an indestructible union of destructible States, the mirror image of the American formula. |
| Emergency and rights architecture | **1.** A full Part XVIII: National Emergency (Art 352), President's Rule (Art 356) and Financial Emergency (Art 360).<br>**2.** Fundamental Rights in Part III alongside non justiciable Directive Principles in Part IV. | **1.** No emergency chapter in the Constitution; national emergencies rest on statute, principally the National Emergencies Act, 1976.<br>**2.** A Bill of Rights, the first ten Amendments of 1791, with no equivalent of the Directive Principles. | The Indian emergency provisions came largely from the Government of India Act, 1935 and the Weimar Constitution, not from the USA. |
