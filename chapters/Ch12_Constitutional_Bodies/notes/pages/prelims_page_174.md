SPSC

- **1.** An Act of the State Legislature may confer additional functions on the SPSC, including as respects the services of a local authority or public institution in the State (Art 321)

- **2.** The Governor appoints one of the Members to act as SPSC Chairman during a vacancy or the Chairman's inability to act (Art 316(1A)) Is the advice / report binding? UPSC

- **1.** No. UPSC advice is advisory only.

- **2.** State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava (1957): the SC held that Art 320(3)(c) is directory, not mandatory. Failure to consult the UPSC does not by itself invalidate a government order and gives the civil servant no cause of action.

- **3.** The only safeguard is political. The annual report must be laid before Parliament with a memorandum explaining every case in which the UPSC’s advice was not accepted (Art 323(1)). SPSC

- **1.** No. The same rule applies; the SPSC’s advice does not bind the State Government.

- **2.** The memorandum on non-acceptance of SPSC advice must be laid before the State Legislature (Art 323(2)). CAG

- **1.** No. The CAG’s reports are recommendatory.

- **2.** He has no power to prosecute, punish or recover money; he can only report.

- **3.** Investigating agencies may use his findings, and Parliament acts through the PAC and COPU, but the CAG himself has no judicial or quasi-judicial power [PRELIMS 2012].

- **4.** The Constitution imposes no compliance obligation. But on the PAC’s directions, Ministries must submit Action Taken Notes (ATNs) on his reports, conventionally within 4 months of tabling. Types of CAG audit

- **1.** The CAG’s own Auditing Standards (2017) recognise three types of audit. A. Financial (attest) audit: checks whether the entity’s financial statements and accounts are presented fairly, in line with the applicable financial reporting framework. B. Compliance (regularity) audit: checks whether transactions, activities and information conform, in all material respects, to the governing laws, rules, sanctions and authorities. C. Performance audit: checks whether a programme, scheme or institution delivers economy, efficiency and effectiveness (the “3 Es”).

- **2.** Propriety audit: a lens, not a fourth type

- It asks whether public spending was wise, faithful and economical.

- It is applied within compliance and performance audits.

- Unlike legal and regulatory audit, which is obligatory, it is discretionary. This is why the CAG is criticised as selective in using it.

- **3.** Specialised streams include IT/systems audit and environment audit.

- **4.** Once tabled, reports of all three kinds are examined by the PAC (Union and State accounts) and the COPU (public undertakings). What the CAG audits - and what he does not What he audits

- **1.** Duties are left to Parliament (Art 149). The CAG (DPC) Act, 1971 sets the perimeter.

- **2.** Under that Act, he audits:

- all receipts and expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of the Union, States and UTs with a legislature;

- Contingency Fund and Public Account transactions;

- trading, manufacturing, profit-and-loss and stores and stock accounts;

- bodies and authorities substantially financed by government grants or loans (₹25 lakh or more, making up at least 75% of their expenditure).

- **3.** Government companies: the CAG appoints or re-appoints the statutory auditor under the Companies Act, 2013, issues directions to him, and may conduct a supplementary audit. Government corporations are audited as their governing Acts provide.

- **4.** Any other body entrusted to him by the President or Governor after consulting him.
