| Kihoto Hollohan, 1992 and judicial review of the Speaker decision | **1.** Bench: Decided on 18 February 1992 by a five-judge Constitution Bench, 3:2 majority.<br>**2.** Validity: The Tenth Schedule was upheld as constitutional.<br>**3.** Para 7 struck down: It barred all courts from reviewing disqualification, effectively altering Arts 136, 226 and 227; since it lacked ratification by half the States under the proviso to Art 368(2), it was held invalid.<br>**4.** Severability: The majority severed Para 7 and saved the rest of the Schedule.<br>**5.** Speaker as Tribunal: While deciding under Para 6, the Speaker acts as a tribunal, so judicial review applies, but only on grounds of violation of constitutional mandate, mala fides, breach of natural justice and perversity.<br>**6.** Timing: No judicial review before the Speaker’s decision.<br>**7.** Whip: Disqualification under Para 2(1)(b) limited to votes vital to the party’s policy, like confidence motions | **1.** Applies identically.<br>**2.** Subhash Desai, May 2023: the Speaker must go by the whip of the political party, not of the legislature party, and neither Governor nor Speaker may recognise a rival faction as the party | Applies to a decision of the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha | Applies to a decision of the Chairman of a Council |
| 91st Amendment, 2003: ministry size and the bar on defectors | **1.** Under Art 75(1A), the Council of Ministers, including the PM, cannot exceed 15% of Lok Sabha strength. The same ministerial bar (Art 75(1B)) and bar on remunerative political posts (Art 361B) apply to defecting members. | Under Art 164(1A), ministry size cannot exceed 15% of Assembly strength, but not less than 12, so in small Assemblies the floor of 12 applies.
