### Fundamental Rights

Fundamental rights are a set of basic rights and freedoms that are considered essential for the protection of human dignity, liberty, and equality and are guaranteed to individuals by a constitution. FR are referred to as the "Magna Carta" of India and are delineated in Part III (Article 12 to 35). These rights are defended and guaranteed by the Supreme Court of India.

### Quotes

- **1.** "Fundamental rights are both the shield and sword against State tyranny." - Prof. Upendra Baxi

- **2.** "Freedom of speech and expression is the bulwark of a democratic government. It is the very foundation of democracy." - Dr. Rajendra Prasad

- **3.** "I measure the progress of a community by the degree of progress which women have achieved." -B R Ambedkar - Affirmative Action

- **4.** "Without political freedom, economic freedom is a mere chimera." - Nehru - Balancing FR and DPSP

- **5.** For Conclusion - "Those who would give up Essential Liberty to purchase Temporary Safety, deserve neither Liberty nor Safety." (Benjamin Franklin). Thus, protection and promotion of FR is vital for the well-being of a free and democratic society.

### Features

- **1.** Guaranteed by the Constitution Part III (Articles 12-35) - enforceable by SC & HC (Art. 32 & 226).

- **2.** Primarily act as limitations on State power, preventing arbitrary actions. Eg. Shreya Singhal case

- **3.** Against the State as well as against private persons. (Art. 17, 23)

- **4.** Not absolute - Subject to reasonable restrictions for public order, morality, security, etc.

- **5.** Negative and Positive obligations - Some rights prevent State interference (freedom of speech) while others impose duties to act (abolition of untouchability).

- **6.** Universal in nature - Apply to all citizens, though certain rights (Eg. Art. 19) are exclusive to citizens.

- **7.** Different classes of rights - Cover equality (Articles 14-18), freedoms (Articles 19-22), against exploitation (Articles 23-24), religion (Articles 25-28), cultural & educational (Articles 29-30), and constitutional remedies (Art. 32).

### Limitations and Criticism

- **1.** Not Absolute - subject to reasonable restrictions for public order, morality, and security. Eg. Internet shutdowns in Kashmir.

- **2.** Limited Nature - Do not include social and economic rights necessary for a free and quality life. Eg. Right to employment, right to social security.

- **3.** Suspension during Emergency under Art. 359. (except Articles 20 & 21).

- **4.** Amendable Nature - Eg. 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 removed Right to Property.

- **5.** Accessing justice under Art. 32 or 226 is a challenge for poor or illiterate citizens.

- **6.** The scope of FRs depends heavily on judicial interpretation. Eg. Expansion of Art. 21 (from "procedure established by law" in A.K. Gopalan, 1950 to "just, fair, reasonable" in Maneka Gandhi, 1978).

- **7.** Conflict with Directive Principles - Eg. Champakam Dorairajan v. State of Madras (1951) struck down caste-based reservations as violating Art. 15, later addressed by the First Amendment (1951).

- **8.** Misuse of Restrictions - Vague grounds like "public order" or "decency" allow overreach.

### Role of Judiciary

Guardian of Fundamental Rights - Judiciary acts as the protector and guarantor of FRs under Art. 32 & 226. Eg. Ambedkar called Art. 32 the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. Judicial Review - Ensures that laws and executive actions do not violate FRs. Eg. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) - Parliament cannot amend FRs to destroy the Basic Structure. Expansive Interpretation

- **1.** Eg. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) - Due process made part of personal liberty.

- **2.** Eg. K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) - Recognised Right to Privacy. Creation of New Rights - Judicial activism has expanded the scope of FRs.

- **1.** Eg. Right to Education (Unnikrishnan v. State of A.P., 1993 → later Art. 21A).

- **2.** Eg. Right to Clean Environment (Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, 1991).

- **3.** Eg. Right to Internet Access (Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, 2020). Striking Down Unconstitutional Laws

- **1.** Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) - Struck down Sec. 66A IT Act for violating free speech.

- **2.** Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) - Decriminalised homosexuality (Sec. 377 IPC). Public Interest Litigation - Expanded access to justice by allowing social activists and NGOs to file for protection of FRs. Eg. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) - Recognised right to speedy trial

### Critical analysis of the role of judiciary

- **1.** Judicial Overreach - Courts intrude into policy-making, raising separation of powers concerns. Eg. SC ban on firecrackers (2017), SC's regulation of BS-VI fuel deadlines (2018).

- **2.** Inherent Judicial Subjectivity - Eg. Doctrine of Basic Structure - Varied interpretations affecting predictability.

- **3.** Accessibility Barriers - Eg. NALSA reports (2023) show over 70% rural poor lack effective legal aid.

### Right to Equality

### Uniform Civil Code

It is a single set of laws governing personal matters like marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption, and maintenance. Article 44 of DPSP of the Constitution states that the 'State shall endeavor to secure a Uniform Civil Code for the citizens throughout the territory of India.' In his Independence Day speech, the Prime Minister called for a Uniform Civil Code, advocating a secular civil framework to replace the existing religion-based personal laws.

### Present Status of Civil Laws in India

- **1.** Hindu Personal Laws - Codified under four Acts: Hindu Marriage Act (1955), Hindu Succession Act (1956), Hindu Minority & Guardianship Act (1956), and Hindu Adoptions & Maintenance Act (1956). Applies also to Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists.

- **2.** Muslim Personal Laws - Uncodified, based on religious texts; partly recognised under the Shariat Act (1937) and Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act (1939).

- **3.** Christian Personal Laws - Governed by the Indian Christian Marriage Act (1872).

- **4.** Secular Laws - Special Marriage Act (1954) allows inter-religious marriages; Guardians and Wards Act (1890) provides a secular guardianship framework.

- **5.** Regional Exceptions - Art. 371 grants exemptions to states like Nagaland, Mizoram, Assam for customary laws.

- **6.** Uniform Codes - Goa and Uttarakhand have adopted a UCC; states like Assam and Gujarat are exploring feasibility.

### Need for UCC

- **1.** Constitutional Duty - Art. 44 directs the state to secure UCC.

- **2.** Addressing Social Divisions - Eg. Polygamy under Shariat Act (1937) still legal for Muslims, but discriminatory against women.

- **3.** Equality under Law - Ensures equal rights for all citizens irrespective of religion, in line with Art. 14. Eg. Uttarakhand UCC (2024) bans polygamy and standardises marriage age at 21.

- **4.** Empowering Women - Removes patriarchal and discriminatory practices like triple talaq, unequal inheritance, child marriage.

- **5.** Simplifying Legal System - Replaces multiple personal laws with one uniform law, reducing judicial backlog and making rights clearer.

- **6.** National Integration - Strengthens common citizenship over religious identity; promotes constitutional patriotism (Habermas).

- **7.** Modernisation & Social Reform - Updates outdated practices and aligns laws with contemporary values. Eg. Navtej Johar (2018) decriminalised same-sex relations.

- **8.** International Alignment - Brings India closer to global human rights standards.
