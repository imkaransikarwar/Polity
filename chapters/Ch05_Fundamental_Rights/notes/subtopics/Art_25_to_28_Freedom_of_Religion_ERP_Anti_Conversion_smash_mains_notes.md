### Anti-Conversion Laws

Anti-conversion laws are the legislations that restrict or prohibit individuals from converting from one religion to another. In India, more than 10 states have anti-conversion laws. Article 25 guarantees freedom of conscience and right to profess, practice, and propagate religion. But this right is not absolute, subject to public order, morality, health, and other fundamental rights.

### Evolution of Anti-Conversion Laws

- **1.** Pre-Independence: Several princely states (Eg. Raigarh, Kota, Patna, Bikaner) enacted anti-conversion laws in the 1930s-40s.

- **2.** Post-Independence State Laws: Since Parliament never passed a national law, states legislated under Entry 1 (public order) and Entry 2 (criminal law) of State List.

### Key Provisions in State Laws

- **1.** Mandatory prior permission or intimation to district magistrate for religious conversion.

- **2.** Ban on conversion through force, fraud, inducement, or allurement.

- **3.** Prescribed penalties (1-10 years imprisonment, fines up to ₹50,000 or more).

- **4.** Special protection for women, minors, and SC/ST individuals.

- **5.** Burden of proof is often placed on the person converting or the religious leader.

### Judicial Pronouncements

- **1.** Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977) - SC upheld state laws, ruling that the right to "propagate" religion does not include the right to "convert" another.

- **2.** Hadiya Case (Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M., 2018) - SC upheld adult woman's right to choose religion and marriage partner.

### Significance

- **1.** Reflects the balance between individual liberty and state interest in public order.

- **2.** Prevents forced, fraudulent, or induced conversions, ensuring genuine choice. Eg. The 1977 Stainislaus judgment noted that "propagate" does not include the right to convert others forcibly.

- **3.** Safeguards Vulnerable Groups - Protects women, minors, and SC/STs from exploitation.

- **4.** Regulates Conversion by Marriage - Prevents concealment or coercion in interfaith marriages.

- **5.** Protecting Public Order: Coercive conversions can trigger social unrest, communal tensions, and localized violence between different religious groups.

- **6.** Cultural Preservation: Protects the indigenous cultural and religious identity of Adivasi/tribal populations from being erased by external missionary activities.

- **7.** Curbing "Love Jihad": Prevents marriages conducted solely for the purpose of religious conversion through deception or misrepresentation.

- **8.** National Security: Concerns that mass conversions funded by foreign NGOs could be used to destabilize border regions or influence politics.

- **9.** Countering Allurement: Prevents "material inducements" like free education or healthcare from being used as bait for switching religions.

### Criticism of Anti-Conversion Laws

- **1.** Infringement of Privacy: Eg. Mandatory notification to a District Magistrate violates the "Right to Privacy" established in the Puttaswamy judgment.

- **2.** Vague Terms - Words like "allurement" and "inducement" are loosely defined, enabling misuse.

- **3.** Violation of Fundamental Rights - Curtails Article 25 (religious freedom) and Article 21 (personal liberty & privacy).

- **4.** Targeted Enforcement - Disproportionately affects minorities and interfaith couples.

- **5.** Politicisation - Often linked to identity politics and communal polarisation.

- **6.** Incentivizing Vigilantism: Laws embolden fringe groups to disrupt interfaith weddings or prayer meetings under the guise of "stopping crime."

- **7.** Burden of Proof: Reversing the burden onto the accused (the convert/priest) is inconsistent with the fundamental legal principle of "innocence until proven guilty."

### Way Forward

- **1.** Uniform Principles - Clear national guidelines to prevent misuse while safeguarding genuine freedom of choice.

- **2.** Judicial Safeguards - Ensure laws do not curtail rights under Articles 21 and 25.

### Essential Religious Practices

- **1.** Recently, SC constituted a 9-judge bench to review the 2018 Sabarimala verdict focusing on the scope of religious freedom (Article 25) versus denominational rights (Article 26).

- **2.** Thiruparankundram Deepathoon Dispute: Madras HC warned officials of contempt for obstructing the Karthigai Deepam ritual atop the hill. It ruled that the state cannot use "law and order" concerns as a pretext to block long-standing religious rituals.

- **3.** Thenkalai Sect Rights: Reaffirming a century-old legal position, the Court ruled that only the Thenkalai sect has the exclusive right to recite specific hymns (Mantrams/Prabandhams) at the Kancheepuram Varadaraja Perumal Temple. It held that an individual's right to worship (Article 25) does not override the established denominational and ritual office rights of a sect protected under Article 26.

### Major Judgments of Judiciary Involvement

- **1.** Shirur Mutt Case(1954): Established the Essential Religious Practices (ERP) test, protecting essential or core rituals while allowing State regulation of secular temple administration.

- **2.** Durgah Committee Case (1961): Ruled that "superstitious beliefs" or "unessential accretions" are not protected under Article 26.

- **3.** Sastri Yagnapurushadji (1966): Defined the scope of "Hinduism" to include diverse sects, preventing them from using "denominational" status to exclude others from temples.

- **4.** Seshammal Case (1972): Held that while the performance of rituals is religious, the appointment of priests is a secular function that the State can regulate.

- **5.** Ananda Marga Case (2004): Clarified that a practice is "essential" only if its absence fundamentally alters the religion itself.

- **6.** Acharya Jagadishwarananda Avadhuta case(2004): Introduced the "essentiality test". A practice is essential only if its absence fundamentally alters the religion.

- **7.** Haji Ali Dargah Case(2016): Struck down the ban on women entering the sanctum

- **8.** Shayara Bano Case(2017)

- **9.** Sabarimala Case(2018)

### Major Doctrines

- **1.** Doctrine of Essentiality: Only those practices that are "integral" or "fundamental" to a religion receive protection under Article 25.

- **2.** Constitutional Morality: The idea that the Constitution's core values (justice, liberty, equality) must prevail over traditional or religious norms if they conflict.

- **3.** Doctrine of Principled Distance: The state does not strictly separate from religion but intervenes only when religious practices infringe upon fundamental rights or social welfare.

- **4.** Doctrine of Pith and Substance: Used to determine if a law truly regulates a "secular" aspect of religion or is an unconstitutional strike at the "religious" core.

- **5.** Definitional Filter: The court first assesses if a claim is "religious" in nature (theistic vs. non-theistic) before applying other tests.

### Justification of Judicial Oversight

- **1.** Protection of Fundamental Rights: Courts intervene when religious customs violate Article 14 (Equality), ensuring traditions do not override rights.

- **2.** Public Health and Peace: Eg. In Church of God (2000), the court justified restricting loudspeakers, ruling that no religion grants the right to disturb the peace of others.

- **3.** Purging Social Evils: Oversight helps eliminate harmful traditions from religious spaces. Eg. Sabarimala

- **4.** Upholding Constitutional Morality: Pratap Bhanu Mehta argues that the Court acts as a "theologian" to modernize religious identity.

- **5.** Defining Secular vs. Religious: It distinguishes between spiritual rites and administrative functions like managing temple funds.

- **6.** Standardizing Legal Definitions: Eg. The Bombay High Court defined religion as anything that "binds a man to his own conscience".

- **7.** Gender Justice in Personal Law: Judicial intervention has been the primary driver for reform in discriminatory divorce or inheritance laws. Eg. Shayara Bano case.

- **8.** Curbing Superstitious Crimes like ritual sacrifice by labeling them "unessential accretions" rather than protected faith. Eg. Durgah Committee Case

- **9.** Refinement of Faith: Judicial "entry into the sanctum" helps refine religions by tethering them to modern democratic values.

### Criticism of Judicial Oversight

- **1.** Judicial Overreach: Secular judges often lack the theological expertise to interpret sacred scriptures and define "essential" rituals correctly.

- **2.** Arbitrary Standards: The ERP test is criticized for being inconsistent, relying on a judge's personal philosophy rather than objective theology.

- **3.** Threat to Autonomy: Constant oversight undermines the right of religious denominations (Art. 26) to manage their internal affairs without interference.

- **4.** Political Sensitivity: Judicial intervention in sensitive religious disputes can inadvertently trigger communal disharmony or perceptions of state bias.

- **5.** Subjective Morality: Using Constitutional Morality is seen as a vague tool used to bypass established religious customs and local practices.

- **6.** Risk of Politicization: Religious rulings are frequently weaponized by political actors to deepen social and communal divisions.

- **7.** Burden of Proof: The ERP doctrine forces believers to "prove" their faith in court rather than the state justifying its restrictions.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Adopting Constitutional Morality: Moving from the "Essentiality" test to a focus on individual dignity and equality.

- **2.** Principled Non-Intervention: Establishing a high threshold for state interference in non-discriminatory, private religious matters.

- **3.** Engaging Religious Experts: Using Amicus Curiae from diverse religious backgrounds to provide nuanced theological context.

- **4.** Encouraging Alternative Dispute Resolution for sectarian conflicts instead of endless court litigation.

- **5.** Empowering Community Reform: Encouraging religions to reform from within rather than through "top-down" judicial mandates.

- **6.** Transparency in State Management: Ensuring state-run temple boards are inclusive and represent the actual worshipping community.
