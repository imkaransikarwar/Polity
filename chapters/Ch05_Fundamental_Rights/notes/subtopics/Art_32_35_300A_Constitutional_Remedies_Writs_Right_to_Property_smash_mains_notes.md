### Right to Property

The Right to Property under the Indian Constitution was derived from Section 299 of GoI Act, 1935, and Article 17 of UDHR,1948. Key Constitutional Amendments

- **1.** 1st Amendment (1951): Inserted Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule to protect land reform laws from judicial review on the grounds of violating fundamental rights.

- **2.** 4th Amendment (1955): Stated that the adequacy of compensation provided by the State for acquiring property could not be challenged in court.

- **3.** 25th Amendment (1971): Replaced the word "compensation" with "amount," further limiting the judiciary's power to scrutinize the value paid for seized property.

- **4.** 44th Amendment (1978): It repealed Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31, removing the Right to Property as a Fundamental Right and re-inserting it as Article 300A (a mere legal right).

  - **a.** Amended Article 30 with new clause (1-A) with the effect that if the state acquires the property of any minority-administered educational institution, the compensation shall not encroach on the rights of the minorities.

### Major Supreme Court Judgments

- **1.** Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (2024) Ruled that not all private property is a "material resource of the community" for state acquisition. It overruled the decades-old "Justice Krishna Iyer" doctrine (which favored total state control), stating that the State cannot indiscriminately seize private property unless it meets specific criteria like scarcity or public utility.

- **2.** Samiullah v. State of Bihar (2026) Quashed rules requiring proof of mutation for registration, emphasizing that the state cannot block property transfers.

- **3.** Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal - Right to Property is a "Human Right" in a welfare state. State cannot be a "trespasser" on its citizens' land and must follow the due process of law for any acquisition.

- **4.** R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) Struck down bank nationalization for providing "illusory" compensation, forcing the state to follow fair valuation principles.

- **5.** Bela Banerjee v. State of West Bengal (1954) - The Court held that compensation meant a "just equivalent" or the full market value of the property.

- **6.** K.T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2011) Held that deprivation of property under Article 300A must follow valid law and be for public purpose.

### Major Challenges in Right to Property in India

- **1.** Presumptive Titling System: Registration proves a transaction occurred, but the government does not guarantee actual ownership of the land. NITI Aayog has suggested shift to "conclusive land titling"

- **2.** Pending Litigation Backlog: Eg. In 2026, roughly 7.3 million property cases remain pending, representing 66% of all civil litigation.

- **3.** Inaccurate and outdated land records - lead to fraudulent sales and overlapping claims.

- **4.** Eminent Domain Misuse: The State sometimes acquires land for "public purpose" that eventually benefits private developers or corporations.

- **5.** Complex Tenant Laws: Archaic Rent Control Acts make it difficult for landlords to evict non-paying or violating tenants.

- **6.** Bargaining Power Asymmetry: Small farmers lack the legal resources to fight state or corporate entities for fair compensation.

- **7.** Gender Bias in Inheritance: Eg. only 13% of rural land titles are held solely by women.

### Right to Property in Other Countries

- **1.** USA: Protected by the 5th Amendment, which states that private property shall not be taken for public use without "just compensation."

- **2.** Australia: The government uses "Conclusive Titling" (Torrens Title), meaning the State guarantees ownership, effectively eliminating title-related litigation.

### Article 32, 226

"It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it." Dr Ambedkar

- **1.** Article 32 and 226: Guarantees the right to move the SC and HC respectively for the enforcement of FR. Empowers the SC to issue directions, orders & writs

- **2.** 32(3): Allows Parliament to empower any other court to exercise these powers within their local limits.

- **3.** 32(4): States that the right to move the SC shall not be suspended except during a National Emergency.

### WRITS

- **1.** Habeas Corpus (To have the body): Used to release a person who has been detained or imprisoned unlawfully. Eg. ADM Jabalpur Case

- **2.** Mandamus (We command): Issued to a public official, corporation, or lower court to perform a mandatory duty they have failed or refused to do. Eg. In Gujarat State Financial Corp v. Lotus Hotels (1983), SC issued a writ of Mandamus to provide loan to a hotel,

- **3.** Prohibition (To forbid): Issued by a higher court to a lower court or quasi-judicial body to stop them from continuing a case that exceeds their jurisdiction.

- **4.** Certiorari (To be certified): Issued to quash or overturn an order already passed by a lower court or authority because it lacked jurisdiction or contained a legal error. Eg. in A.K. Kraipak case, SC quashed a selection list for the Forest Service as one of the members of the selection board was also a candidate.

- **5.** Quo Warranto (By what authority): Issued to prevent a person from illegally holding a public office and to inquire into the legality of their claim to that office.

### Comparison between SC & HC Powers

| Feature | Supreme Court (Article 32) | High Court (Article 226) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature of Right | The SC cannot refuse to exercise this power. | Discretionary Power. The HC may refuse to grant relief if an alternative remedy exists. |
| Scope of Power | Narrower: Can only be invoked for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights (Part III). | Wider: Can be invoked for Fundamental Rights plus ordinary legal rights. |

### Significance

- **1.** Article 32 is a Fundamental Right. Ambedkar called it the "Heart and Soul" because rights are meaningless without a direct enforcement mechanism.

- **2.** Public Interest Litigation allows any citizen to approach courts for the protection of marginalized groups' rights. Eg. M C Mehta cases on environment

- **3.** Democratic Stability: By providing a legal vent for grievances, they prevent citizens from resorting to extra-constitutional means for justice.

- **4.** They provide a direct shield against the abuse of power, ensuring that fundamental rights are not just promises on paper but actionable guarantees.

- **5.** They act as a constitutional check and balance, forcing the executive and legislature to operate strictly within the boundaries of the law.

- **6.** They foster a culture of accountability and justice by allowing the marginalized to challenge systemic inequalities through Public Interest Litigation (PIL).

### Major Challenges

- **1.** Exponential Misuse: Eg. Justice Nagarathna flagged the "gross misuse" of Article 32 for seeking simple court adjournments.

- **2.** Massive Case Pendency: Eg. over 6 million cases pending in HCs, with writs forming a significant chunk.

- **3.** Executive Non-compliance: Eg. A 2026 report noted a 15% rise in contempt cases against state officials for ignoring Mandamus orders.

- **4.** Geographical Barriers: While Article 32 is Pan-India, traveling to Delhi is prohibitively expensive for citizens from remote southern or eastern regions.

- **5.** Cost of Litigation: High legal fees for senior advocates in writ matters make these remedies inaccessible to the poor.

- **6.** Vigilante Intimidation: Eg. attacks on RTI/PIL activists who filed writs against illegal mining.

- **7.** Over-reach Concerns: Eg. The Law Minister in 2025 suggested that writ jurisdiction should not interfere in "complex economic policy decisions."

### Major SC judgments

- **1.** L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) - power of judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 is part of the "Basic Structure" of the Constitution.

- **2.** Kishore Samrite v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2013) - The SC established strict guidelines to prevent the abuse of the process of court through frivolous Article 32 petitions.

- **3.** S.S. Ahluwalia v. Union of India - SC can award compensation under Article 32 for the violation of fundamental rights by the State. Validity of Narco Tests The SC recently clarified the legal boundaries of narco-tests in Amlesh Kumar v. State of Bihar (2025).
