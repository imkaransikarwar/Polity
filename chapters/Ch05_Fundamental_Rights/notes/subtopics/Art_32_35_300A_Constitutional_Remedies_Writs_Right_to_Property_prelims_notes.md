PART III - FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS: GENERAL FEATURES Nature and Scope

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** Contained in Part III of the Constitution; described as the Magna Carta of the Indian Constitution.

- **2.** Place limits on the tyranny of the Executive and on arbitrary laws of the Legislature.

- **3.** Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights.

---

### [Prelims Source Page 55](../../pages/prelims/prelims_page_055.pdf)

- **3.** Institutions recognised by the State: religious instruction permitted on a voluntary basis.

- **4.** Institutions receiving aid from the State: religious instruction permitted on a voluntary basis. CULTURAL AND EDUCATIONAL RIGHTS (ART 29-30)

### Article 29 - Protection of interests of minorities

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** Any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve the same.

- **2.** No citizen can be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State, or receiving aid out of State funds, on grounds of religion, race, caste or language.

- **3.** Covers both religious and linguistic minorities.

- **4.** The Constitution of India does not define minorities. EXCEPTIONS / LAWS / JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION Supreme Court judgments:

- **1.** The right to conserve the language includes the right to agitate for the protection of the language.

- **2.** The scope of this Article is not necessarily restricted to minorities alone.

- **3.** This is because of the use of the words 'section of citizens' in Article 29, which includes minorities as well as the majority.

### Article 30 - Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions [PRELIMS 2011]

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** Right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

- **2.** The State shall not discriminate against minority-managed institutions while granting aid.

- **3.** Available only to religious and linguistic minorities, not to any section of citizens. EXCEPTIONS / LAWS / JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION

- **1.** Contrast with Article 29, which extends to any section of citizens; Article 30 is confined to minorities.

- **2.** Compensation for compulsory acquisition of the property of a minority educational institution must not restrict or abrogate the right guaranteed under Article 30. RIGHT TO CONSTITUTIONAL REMEDIES (ART 32) AND ART 35

### Article 32 - Right to Constitutional Remedies

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** Entitles citizens to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

- **2.** Described by Dr B.R. Ambedkar as the heart and soul of the Constitution.

- **3.** Writs are written orders issued by the Supreme Court to provide constitutional remedies and protect the fundamental rights of citizens from violation.

- **4.** Five writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto. EXCEPTIONS / LAWS / JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION Exceptions and limits:

- **1.** The right to move the Supreme Court shall not be suspended except as provided for by the Constitution, not by Parliament.

- **2.** Accordingly, the Constitution provides that the President can suspend enforcement of Fundamental Rights during a National Emergency (Article 359).

- **3.** Violation of a Fundamental Right is a sine qua non for Article 32; it cannot be invoked to determine the constitutionality of an executive order or legislation unless it directly infringes a Fundamental Right.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature of right | A Fundamental Right. | A constitutional right. |
| Scope | Narrow scope. Applicable for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights alone. | Broader scope. Applicable for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights as well as legal rights. |
| Jurisdiction | Broader territorial jurisdiction; the Supreme Court can issue writs all over India. | Narrower territorial jurisdiction; a High Court can issue a writ within its own local jurisdiction only. |
| Discretion to refuse | Since Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right, the Supreme Court cannot refuse relief. | Article 226 confers a discretionary power on the High Court, which may or may not issue a writ. |
| Suspension | Art 32(4): the right to move the Supreme Court cannot be suspended, except as the Constitution itself provides. The one route: during a National Emergency, the President may suspend enforcement of Fundamental Rights under Article 359. Even then, Arts 20 and 21 cannot be suspended (44th Amendment, 1978). | Cannot be suspended even at the time of emergency. |

| WRIT | PURPOSE | CAN BE ISSUED AGAINST |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Habeas Corpus | To direct the release of a person detained unlawfully. | Both public authorities and private individuals. |
| Mandamus | To direct a public authority to perform its duty. | A public body, a corporation, an inferior court, a tribunal, or the government. Not against a private individual, the President or Governors, or the Chief Justice of a High Court acting judicially. |
| Prohibition | To prohibit a lower court or tribunal from proceeding in a case exceeding its jurisdiction. | Judicial and quasi-judicial authorities only. Not against administrative authorities, legislative bodies or private individuals. |

### Article 35 - Legislation to give effect to Fundamental Rights

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** The power to give effect to certain specified Fundamental Rights vests only in Parliament and not in the State legislatures.

- **2.** This ensures uniformity throughout India regarding the nature of those Fundamental Rights and the punishment for their infringement. EXCEPTIONS / LAWS / JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION Parliament alone may make laws for:

- **1.** Prescribing residence as a condition for certain employment or appointments in a State, Union Territory, local authority or other authority.

- **2.** Empowering courts other than the Supreme Court and the High Courts to issue directions, orders and writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

- **3.** Restricting or abrogating the application of Fundamental Rights to members of the armed forces, police forces, etc.

- **4.** Indemnifying any government servant or other person for acts done during the operation of martial law in any area.

### ALLIED THEMES

Rights outside Part III [PRELIMS 2021, 2017]

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** Right to property ceased to be a Fundamental Right when the 44th Amendment Act, 1978 omitted Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31.

- **2.** It now rests in Article 300A, Part XII: 'No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law', that is a legal or constitutional right available to any person, citizen or foreigner, and not to citizens alone.

- **3.** Right to vote and to be elected is not a Fundamental Right. It flows from Article 326 (adult suffrage) and is regulated by the Representation of the People Act, 1951, making it a constitutional right exercised subject to statutory conditions. EXCEPTIONS / LAWS / JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION

- **1.** The Supreme Court has held that though no longer a Fundamental Right, property remains a constitutional right under

### Article 300A and a human right in a welfare State, and cannot be taken away except in accordance with law.

- **2.** Consequence: only Part III rights are enforceable under Article 32; these other rights are enforced through ordinary law or under Article 226. Right to Privacy [PRELIMS 2024, 2021, 2018]

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India, 2017: a nine-judge Bench of the Supreme Court held on 24 August 2017 that the right to privacy is a Fundamental Right.

- **2.** Privacy is protected as an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21, and as part of the freedoms guaranteed in Part III.

- **3.** The anchor article is Article 21; it is not placed under Article 15, 16, 19 or 29. EXCEPTIONS / LAWS / JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION

- **1.** It is not an absolute right; it may be restricted only by a fair, just and reasonable procedure established by law.

- **2.** The ruling is the constitutional basis of India's data protection law, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. UDHR, 1948 and the Constitution [PRELIMS 2020, 2011]

### CORE PROVISIONS

- **1.** The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted on 10 December 1948 and has 30 articles; India took part in its drafting.

- **2.** Besides the Fundamental Rights, its principles and provisions are reflected in the Preamble, the Directive Principles of State Policy and the Fundamental Duties, all three.

- **3.** The civil and political rights of the UDHR broadly correspond to Part III; its social, economic and cultural rights correspond to Part IV. EXCEPTIONS / LAWS / JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION

- **1.** Rights expressly recognised in the UDHR include Article 26, the right to education; Article 21(2), the right of equal access to public service in one's country; and Article 25(1), an adequate standard of living including food, that is the right to food.

- **2.** The UDHR is a declaration, not a treaty: it is not by itself legally binding, but it informs the reading of Part III.
