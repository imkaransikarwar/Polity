### Right to Property

The Right to Property under the Indian Constitution was derived from Section 299 of GoI Act, 1935, and Article 17 of UDHR,1948. Key Constitutional Amendments

- **1.** 1st Amendment (1951): Inserted Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule to protect land reform laws from judicial review on the grounds of violating fundamental rights.

- **2.** 4th Amendment (1955): Stated that the adequacy of compensation provided by the State for acquiring property could not be challenged in court.

- **3.** 25th Amendment (1971): Replaced the word "compensation" with "amount," further limiting the judiciary's power to scrutinize the value paid for seized property.

- **4.** 44th Amendment (1978): It repealed Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31, removing the Right to Property as a Fundamental Right and re-inserting it as Article 300A (a mere legal right).

  - **a.** Amended Article 30 with new clause (1-A) with the effect that if the state acquires the property of any minority-administered educational institution, the compensation shall not encroach on the rights of the minorities.

### Major Supreme Court Judgments

- **1.** Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (2024) Ruled that not all private property is a "material resource of the community" for state acquisition. It overruled the decades-old "Justice Krishna Iyer" doctrine (which favored total state control), stating that the State cannot indiscriminately seize private property unless it meets specific criteria like scarcity or public utility.

- **2.** Samiullah v. State of Bihar (2026) Quashed rules requiring proof of mutation for registration, emphasizing that the state cannot block property transfers.

- **3.** Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal - Right to Property is a "Human Right" in a welfare state. State cannot be a "trespasser" on its citizens' land and must follow the due process of law for any acquisition.

- **4.** R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) Struck down bank nationalization for providing "illusory" compensation, forcing the state to follow fair valuation principles.

- **5.** Bela Banerjee v. State of West Bengal (1954) - The Court held that compensation meant a "just equivalent" or the full market value of the property.

- **6.** K.T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2011) Held that deprivation of property under Article 300A must follow valid law and be for public purpose.

### Major Challenges in Right to Property in India

- **1.** Presumptive Titling System: Registration proves a transaction occurred, but the government does not guarantee actual ownership of the land. NITI Aayog has suggested shift to "conclusive land titling"

- **2.** Pending Litigation Backlog: Eg. In 2026, roughly 7.3 million property cases remain pending, representing 66% of all civil litigation.

- **3.** Inaccurate and outdated land records - lead to fraudulent sales and overlapping claims.
