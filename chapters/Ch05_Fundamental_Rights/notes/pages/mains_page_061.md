### Challenges in OTT regulation

- **1.** Jurisdictional Overlap - Turf war between MeiTY, MIB, and TRAI creates confusion over who should regulate OTTs.

- **2.** Balancing Free Speech & Regulation - OTT is a pull medium, unlike broadcast, making excessive censorship a chilling effect on Article 19(1)(a).

- **3.** Vague Standards - Terms like "obscene," "immoral," "offensive" are context-dependent, risking arbitrary interpretation.

- **4.** Content Diversity - OTT platforms host regional, global, experimental content, making uniform classification and regulation difficult.

- **5.** Enforcement Weakness - IT Rules, 2021 rely on self-regulation but lack teeth for enforcement and appeal mechanisms for publishers.

- **6.** Tech vs. Traditional Models - Borrowing censorship models from TV/films ignores the on-demand and internet-based nature of OTT.

- **7.** Global Platforms vs. Indian Laws - Multinational OTTs (Netflix, Amazon Prime) operate across borders, complicating compliance with Indian regulatory frameworks.

- **8.** Data & Privacy Concerns - OTT platforms collect massive user data; regulation must balance data protection with innovation.

- **9.** Political & Social Pressures - Content controversies (Eg. Tandav series, 2021) show how religious/cultural sensitivities often lead to calls for bans.

- **10.** Technological Limitations - Automated filters and content scanners may lead to over-blocking or under-filtering, harming both creativity and user safety.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Tripartite Expertise - Create a specialised body drawing from IT, broadcasting, and telecom sectors to draft bespoke OTT regulations, reflecting both infrastructure and content aspects.

- **2.** Co-Regulation Model - Retain self-regulation by platforms while adding independent oversight to ensure flexibility, accountability, and stakeholder participation.

- **3.** Clear Definitions - Avoid vague terms like "grossly offensive" (as in struck-down Section 66A IT Act). Clearly define harmful, obscene, and offensive content to prevent arbitrary censorship.

- **4.** Judicial Oversight - Ensure that blocking or takedown orders require judicial review, preventing misuse of executive powers for censorship.

- **5.** Evolving Standards - Recognise that morality and obscenity are context-driven; include review clauses for periodic reassessment of content norms.

- **6.** Inclusivity in Regulation - Incorporate voices of marginalised and vulnerable groups to prevent silencing of critical depictions of caste, gender, or social evils.

- **7.** Stakeholder Consultations - Involve platforms, creators, civil society, academia, and government in shaping balanced rules that respect free speech and societal sensitivities.

- **8.** Soft-Touch Framework - Focus on age-based classification, content labelling, parental controls, rather than heavy-handed censorship models like CBFC.

- **9.** Appeal Mechanism - Amend gaps in IT Rules, 2021 by providing publishers a right to appeal decisions of self-regulating bodies, ensuring fairness.
