## Chapter 6
Directive Principles Of State Policy
DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES OF STATE POLICY
1. Discuss the possible factors that inhibit India from enacting for its citizens a uniform civil code as
provided for in the Directive Principles of State Policy. (2015)
Directive Principles of State Policy are enumerated in Part IV of the Constitution from Articles 36 to 51. Dr
B.R. Ambedkar described these principles as 'novel features' of the Indian Constitution.
Granville Austin has described the Directive Principles and the Fundamental Rights as the 'Conscience of the
Constitution'.
It promotes the principles of social and economic democracy and envisages establishing a welfare state.
Directive Principles together with the Fundamental Rights contain the "philosophy of the Constitution" and
are known as the "soul of the Constitution".
Reason why DPSP are non-justiciable
1. Fiscal Constraints - Eg. Providing a universal "Right to Work" (Article 41) would have bankrupted the
state treasury in 1950.
2. Administrative Limitations - The state machinery was initially insufficient to manage complex social
reforms like universal education or health infrastructure.
3. Vast geographical and social diversity made uniform application of certain principles difficult and
potentially socially disruptive. Eg. "Uniform Civil Code" (Article 44)
4. Political Sanction vs. Judicial Sanction - Dr. Ambedkar argued that the real sanction behind DPSPs is
"public opinion" and the "ballot box," not the courtroom.
5. Flexibility for Governance - Non-justiciability allows the government to prioritize goals based on the
prevailing socio-economic climate and urgent national needs.
6. Potential for Institutional Conflict - Making DPSPs justiciable would have led to constant friction
between the Judiciary and the Executive over policy-making priorities.
7. Evolutionary Nature of Rights - Many DPSP goals were intended to "mature" into Fundamental Rights
as the nation progressed and resources grew. Eg. "Right to Education"
8. DPSPs serve as a "Moral Code" for the state, guiding the conscience of legislators rather than imposing
rigid legal chains
9. Preventive Legal Overload on judiciary
Amendments in DPSP
42nd Amendment Act, 1976
1. Article 39(f): opportunity for children to develop in a healthy manner and protection against
exploitation.
2. Article 39A: Free Legal Aid to the poor
3. Article 43A: steps to secure Worker Participation in Management of industries.
4. Article 48A: protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests & wildlife

Significance of Directive Principles
1. Welfare State: Transitions India from a colonial "police state" to a modern, welfare-oriented democracy
for all citizens. Eg. Schemes like Ayushman Bharat uphold Article 47.
2. Socio-Economic Democracy: ensures that political freedom is backed by social and financial security.
Eg. MGNREGA fulfills the "Right to Work"(Article 41) and "Living Wage"(Article 43)
3. Judicial Interpretative Tool: Eg. The Right to Digital Access was recognized as essential for fulfilling
Article 38's equality mandate in Amar Jain case
4. Policy Continuity: Ensures a common minimum political manifesto that remains consistent even when
governments change after elections.
5. Legislative Roadmap: Acts as a constitutional "to-do list" for Parliament to draft transformative laws for
the marginalized. Eg. Maternity Benefit Act to fulfill Article 42.
6. Economic Justice and Equity: Mandates the state to prevent the concentration of wealth and ensure fair
distribution of resources.
7. International Relations Guide: Eg. India's active role in UN Peacekeeping and climate pacts aligns with
the mandate of Article 51.
8. Environmental Stewardship: Eg. The Project Lion and Project Tiger initiatives are practical
implementations of the directives in Article 48A.
9. Democratic Accountability: Allows the electorate and the Opposition to evaluate a government's
performance based on constitutional benchmarks.
Criticism of Directive Principles
1. Lack of Legal Force: Being non-justiciable, they are often dismissed as "mere pious wishes" or moral
precepts without teeth. Eg. gender pay gap
2. Illogical Arrangement: Critics like Ivor Jennings noted that DPSPs are not logically classified or based
on a consistent philosophy.
3. Ambiguity and Vagueness: Eg. "Adequate means of livelihood" (Article 39a) lacks a quantitative
definition.
4. Incompatibility with Federalism: The Centre can issue directions to states to implement DPSPs; failure
may lead to "President's Rule."
5. Drain on Exchequer: Eg. "Prohibition" (Article 47) as a loss of essential excise revenue.

6. Vulnerability to Political Populism: Governments may use DPSPs as a shield to pass populist laws that
infringe on individual liberties. Eg. UCC
7. Judicial Overreach: Scope for "inventing" new rights by reading DPSPs into FRs.
8. Tyranny of the Majority: Critics of Article 44 (Uniform Civil Code) fear it may be used to impose
majority culture on minorities.
Conflict Between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State
Policy
1. Champakam Dorairajan Case (1951): The SC ruled that Fundamental Rights (FR) are superior to DPSP. If
they conflict, FR would prevail, and DPSP must run as a "subsidiary" to them.
2. Golaknath Case (1967): Held that FRs are "sacrosanct" and cannot be amended by Parliament even to
implement the Directive Principles.
3. Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973): Overruled Golaknath. It held that Parliament can amend any part of
the Constitution (including FRs) to implement DPSP, provided the Basic Structure is not destroyed.
4. Minerva Mills Case (1980): A landmark ruling that established the "Doctrine of Harmony." The Court
held that the Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between FRs and DPSPs.
Neither has absolute supremacy over the other.
5. Unni Krishnan Case (1993): The SC famously bridged the gap by reading Article 45 (Education) into
Article 21, demonstrating that DPSP can be used to expand FRs.
6. Present Position - Fundamental Rights enjoy supremacy over DPSP except for Articles 39 (b) and (c)
which enjoy supremacy over Articles 14 and 19.
India's achievements
Economic Justice & Livelihood (Articles 38, 39, 41)
1. MGNREGA: Provides a legal "Right to Work" (Article 41), supporting nearly 15 crore households annually.
2. Financial Inclusion (PM Jan Dhan Yojana): Brought over 55 crore citizens into the formal banking
system, preventing the "concentration of wealth" (Article 39c).
3. PM-KISAN: provides direct income support to 11 crore farmers (Article 38).
Social Justice & Legal Aid (Article 39A, 46)
1. NALSA & Lok Adalats: fulfill Article 39A by providing free legal aid. In 2025, Lok Adalats settled over 1.2
crore cases in a single day.
2. Scholarships for SC/ST: In the 2025-26, over ₹7,200 crore was released to 61 lakh SC beneficiaries under
post-matric scholarship schemes (Article 46).
3. EWS Reservation: The 103rd Amendment (2019) ensures educational and economic interests of the
"weaker sections" of the unreserved category.
Grassroots Democracy (Article 40)
1. Panchayati Raj: The 73rd and 74th Amendments.
2. Almost all 2.5 lakh Gram Panchayats are connected via BharatNet, enabling local self-government
through e-governance.
Public Health & Nutrition (Article 47)
1. Ayushman Bharat (PM-JAY): The world's largest health insurance scheme. Covers all senior citizens
aged 70+ (as of late 2025).
2. National Food Security Act: Provides subsidized food grains to 80 crore people
