### Panchayati Raj

"The success of democracy in India depends on the success of democracy at the grassroots through Panchayati Raj." - Rajiv Gandhi "Panchayati Raj embodies the spirit of participatory democracy, enabling citizens to have a direct say in their governance." They are inspired by Gandhiji's concept of "Oceanic Circles of Power" and "Swaraj" To realise the vision of "Gram Uday se Bharat Uday", India needs second-generation Panchayati Raj reforms focused on subsidiarity, fiscal autonomy, and participatory democracy. Subsidiarity principle - decision-making authority is best placed

- Where responsibility for outcomes will occur

- In the closest appropriate proximity to where the actions will be taken

### Significance of Local Bodies

Rajni Kothari - Described local bodies as "schools of democracy" where political awareness and participation are cultivated at the grassroots level.

- **1.** Normative Foundation: It rests on two core values - efficiency in public service delivery and deepening of democracy through proximity to citizens.

- **2.** Democratic Decentralization - Eg. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign grants local bodies control over 40% of the state's plan budget.

- **3.** Principle of Subsidiarity: decisions closer to the people. Eg. selection of beneficiaries under PM-Awas Yojana

- **4.** Inclusivity and social justice: representation to Scheduled Castes, and Scheduled Tribes.

- **5.** Implementation of Schemes, ensuring that benefits reach the grassroots. Eg. MGNREGA.

- **6.** Gender Empowerment: Over 14.5 lakh Elected Women Representatives, with 21 states providing 50% reservation.

### Major Issues faced by PRIs

"PRIs exist as over-structured but underpowered organisations." They act merely as administrative vehicles of state governments, rather than as autonomous institutions of self-governance. (2nd ARC) Funds

- **1.** Dependence on higher tiers: Around 95% of Panchayat funds come from Central/State transfers.

- **2.** Irregular flow of grants: Delays in fund release under Finance Commissions and CSS schemes.

- **3.** Although direct transfers to panchayats rose from ₹1.45 lakh crore (13th FC) to ₹4.35 lakh crore (16th FC), 60% are tied grants

- **4.** Limited own-source revenue: Poor tax collection efficiency (>1% own tax).

- **5.** Weak financial management: corruption, audit delays. (No decentralisation of power, rather decentralisation of corruption - Mani Shankar Iyer Committee)

- **6.** Rising Finance Commission allocations has reduced motivation for OSR mobilisation

- **7.** Barriers to Local Taxation: fear of losing popularity discourages local taxation. Functions

- **1.** Incomplete devolution: Less than 10 States have transferred all 29 subjects under 11th Schedule. (overall Panchayat Devolution Index is only 44%)

- **2.** Centralised Welfare via Cash Transfers (DBT), bypassing panchayats. Eg. Schemes like PM-KISAN exclude them from beneficiary selection

- **3.** Role Ambiguity: Lack of clear "Activity Mapping" leads to overlaps between the three tiers (Gram, Block, and Zila).

- **4.** Lack of planning autonomy: District Planning Committees largely dysfunctional or symbolic.

- **5.** Conditionalities under CSS leaves little scope for local prioritisation. Functionaries

- **1.** Shortage of staff: Average 0.67 Panchayat Secretaries per Gram Panchayat, as low as 0.33 in Uttar Pradesh.

- **2.** Dual control: Local staff often answer to line departments, not Panchayats.

- **3.** Absence of dedicated cadre for Panchayats in majority of states

- **4.** Representatives lack technical training to use digital portals like eGramSwaraj or AuditOnline.

- **5.** The "Sarpanch-Pati" Culture: male relatives exercising real power.

- **6.** Inadequate Infrastructure: Approximately 20% of Panchayats still lack dedicated office buildings (Panchayat Bhawans) or stable internet. Functionality

- **1.** Weak Gram Sabhas: Low participation, elite domination, and token meetings.

- **2.** Poor digital and administrative capacity: Limited use of e-Gram Swaraj and GIS-based planning.

- **3.** Accountability issues: Weak social audit mechanisms and poor grievance redressal.

- **4.** Urbanisation Shift in Policy Focus as the rural population fell from 75% (1990) to about 60%.

- **5.** Lack of real-time data on village assets makes evidence-based planning difficult.

### District Planning Committees

- **1.** The Union Budget 2026-27 proposed mapping "City Economic Regions" (CERs), where DPCs will play a nodal role in coordinating between ULBs and surrounding rural Panchayats.

- **2.** The Ministry of Panchayati Raj issued guidelines for DPCs to incorporate "District Climate Resilience Plans" into the annual draft development plans.

### Constitutional Provisions

- **1.** Article 243ZD: Inserted by the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992.

- **2.** Composition: The State Legislature determines the composition.

  - **a.** At least four-fifths (80%) of the members must be elected by and from amongst the elected members of the district's Panchayats and Municipalities.

  - **b.** The ratio of members from rural and urban areas must be in proportion to the population ratio of the district.

### Key Functions

- **1.** Consolidation of Plans: Merging individual Gram Panchayat Development Plans (GPDP) and Urban Local Body (ULB) plans into one cohesive document.

- **2.** Draft Development Plan: Preparing a comprehensive blueprint for the entire district's social and economic development.

- **3.** Helps in prioritizing and recommending allocation of financial resources for different sectors based on local needs.

- **4.** Spatial Planning: Addressing the physical layout of the district, including zoning and land-use patterns across rural-urban borders.

- **5.** Resource Mapping: Assessing available financial and natural resources (water, minerals, forests) for optimal allocation.

- **6.** Infrastructure Integration: Planning common infrastructure like district highways, solid waste plants, and water pipelines that serve both towns and villages.

- **2.** Bureaucratic Hegemony: Dominated by the District Collector or state-appointed officials.

- **3.** Non-binding Nature of Recommendations

- **4.** DPCs have no independent budget or "taxing power" to execute them.

- **5.** Lack of Technical Staff: lack of town planners, or economists to create "scientific" plans.

- **6.** Parastatal bodies (like Water Boards) execute projects without consulting the DPC.

- **7.** Rural-Urban Disconnect: limited "integrated" planning for peri-urban areas (Rurban clusters).

- **8.** Irregular Meetings

- **9.** Sidelining the Gram Sabha during the "consolidation" phase at the district level.

- **10.** MPs and MLAs dominate discussions, suppressing voices of ward councillor

### Way Forward

- **1.** Independent Secretariat: Every DPC must have a permanent office with professional town planners and economists (Sumit Bose Committee).

- **2.** Financial Devolution: A dedicated "District Planning Fund" should be created, with a share of the State GST being directly credited to DPCs.

- **3.** Mandatory Social Audit: The draft plans must be placed in the public domain for 30 days for citizen feedback before finalization.

- **4.** Regular Meetings, mandatory quorum, and proper documentation.

### PESA Act

PESA Act, 1996 is a landmark legislation that aims to extend the 73rd Amendment to the Fifth Schedule Areas of India. It is called a "Constitution within the Constitution" for tribal people.

### Key Objectives

- **1.** Self-Rule through traditional Gram Sabhas for people living in Scheduled Areas.

- **2.** To recognize and safeguard the traditions, customs, and cultural identity of tribals.

- **3.** Resource Control: ownership of minor forest produce and control over local resources.

- **4.** Prevent Exploitation: protect from land alienation, moneylending, and intoxicant abuse.

- **5.** Participatory Democracy: make the Gram Sabha the nucleus of all developmental activities.

- **6.** To recognize traditional community-based dispute resolution mechanisms.

- **7.** Bottom-up Planning: To ensure that every development project is approved by GS. Adi Karmayogi Abhiyan - national movement to build a decentralized tribal leadership and governance ecosystem.

### Key Provisions of PESA Act

- **1.** Every village shall have a Gram Sabha consisting of persons whose names are included in the electoral rolls.

- **2.** Every Panchayat must obtain a "Certificate of Utilization" from the Gram Sabha for funds used for development plans.

- **3.** Reservation of Seats:

  - **a.** Reservation for STs shall not be less than 50% of the total number of seats.

  - **b.** All Chairpersons at all levels of Panchayats must be from the Scheduled Tribes.

- **4.** Land & Water Control:

  - **a.** Gram Sabha must be consulted before land acquisition for development projects and before resettling affected persons.

  - **b.** Gram Sabhas are empowered to prevent land alienation and restore unlawfully alienated tribal land.

- **5.** The recommendation of the Gram Sabha is mandatory for granting prospective licenses or mining leases for minor minerals in Scheduled Areas.

- **6.** Power to regulate or restrict the sale and consumption of any intoxicant, ownership of minor forest produce, and control over money-lending to STs.

### Major Issues with PESA Act

- **1.** PESA applies only to Scheduled Areas under the Fifth Schedule, leaving out tribal populations in non-scheduled areas, limiting its scope and impact.

- **2.** Rule Notification Lags: Eg. Jharkhand notified rules only in 2026, while Odisha's rules remain in draft form.

- **3.** "Consultation" vs. "Consent": Lack of "prior informed consent." Eg. In the Hasdeo Arand case, Gram Sabha resolutions against mining were bypassed by the administration.

- **4.** Conflict with Central Acts: Laws like the Mines and Minerals Act and Land Acquisition Act often override PESA's protective provisions.

- **5.** Bureaucratic Resistance: Eg. Forest officials in several states continue to harass tribal gatherers despite PESA's ownership clause for MFP.

- **6.** Local elites or non-tribal middlemen often manipulate Gram Sabha meetings to secure fraudulent resolutions for industrial projects.

- **7.** Overlapping Jurisdiction: Confusion between the Forest Rights Act (FRA) 2006 and PESA creates legal stalemates over the definition of "community forest."

- **8.** Limited Technical Capacity: Gram Sabhas lack the staff or expertise to manage complex mining lease recommendations or large budgets.

- **9.** Official Panchayat structures sometimes conflict with traditional tribal headmen (Eg. Manjhi-Pargana system), causing internal friction.

- **10.** Absence of Judicial Recourse: There is no dedicated fast-track tribunal to address PESA violations, forcing tribes into long, expensive litigations.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Xaxa Committee (2014):

  - **a.** Legal harmonization between PESA and all state subject laws.

  - **b.** Empowering Gram Sabhas to cancel illegal land transfers directly.

- **2.** The Center should release a unified Central Model Rule template to prevent states from diluting the Act's intent.

### Case Studies

- **1.** Mendha-Lekha (Maharashtra): The first village in India to win the right to sell Tendu leaves (MFP), becoming a million-rupee economy managed by the Gram Sabha.

- **2.** Niyamgiri (Odisha): In the famous Vedanta Case, 12 Gram Sabhas unanimously voted against bauxite mining, leading the SC to uphold tribal religious rights.

- **3.** Dhar (Madhya Pradesh): Implemented a "Panchayat-plus" model where Gram Sabhas successfully regulated local markets to stop the exploitation of tribal artisans.

### Women Reservation

### Constitutional and Legal Provisions

- **1.** Article 243-D (3): Mandates one-third (33%) reservation.

- **2.** Article 243-D (4): Mandates one-third (33%) reservation for Chairpersons.

- **3.** Within the seats reserved for SC and ST, one-third must be reserved for women.

- **4.** 21 states have legally increased reservation to 50% Globally, only 18 out of 146 countries have achieved over 40% representation of women in local governance.

### Benefits of Women Reservation

- **1.** Social Infrastructure: Women leaders invest more in "soft" infrastructure like drinking water and sanitation.

- **2.** Community Mobilization against social evils like child marriage.

- **3.** Economic Empowerment by integration of SHGs with PRIs. Eg. Kudumbashree model in Kerala.

- **4.** Public presence of women in authority challenges deep-seated patriarchal norms and aspirational barriers.

- **5.** Accountability in Welfare: Women-led PRIs show higher transparency in implementation of welfare schemes..

- **6.** Environmental Stewardship: Women are key stakeholders in forest and water conservation, leading to better resource management.

- **7.** Inclusive Gram Sabhas: encourages more rural women to attend and speak in GSs

- **8.** Political Pipeline: PRIs acts as a training ground for women to enter state and national politics

- **9.** Role Models: Eg. Increased female school enrollment and aspirations due to visible female leaders.

### Limitations and Challenges

- **1.** Sarpanch-Pati Culture: reduces the elected woman to a "proxy" leader.

- **2.** Rotational reservation prevents women from contesting the same seat twice, breaking governance continuity.

- **3.** Low Digital Literacy: Rapid digitalization of PRI accounting poses a hurdle for semi-literate women representatives.

- **4.** Inadequate Training: Generic training modules often fail to address the specific administrative needs of first-time women leaders.

- **5.** Double Burden: Balancing intense administrative duties with traditional household chores limits the time available for governance.

- **6.** Financial Dependence: Lack of independent funds (OSR) makes women leaders dependent on state-appointed male officials (Secretaries).

- **7.** Caste-Gender Intersections: Dalit and Tribal women face "double discrimination" from both upper-caste men and patriarchal structures.

- **8.** Bureaucratic Apathy: State-level officials often treat women leaders with less respect than their male counterparts.

- **9.** Information Gap: Limited access to mainstream news and legal updates keeps many EWRs unaware of new schemes.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Creating a dedicated cadre of Panchayat Secretaries to assist women leaders with technical tasks.

- **2.** Abolish Rotation: Move toward a system where seats are reserved for two consecutive terms (10 years) to allow women to build political capital.

- **3.** Incentivize Performance: The 16th Finance Commission should provide extra grants to Panchayats that show high scores on the "Gender Governance Index."

- **4.** Enact a "Protection of Local Representatives Act" to penalize those who harass or obstruct women leaders in their official duties.

- **5.** Recommendations by panel constituted by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj

  - **a.** 'exemplary penalties' for proven cases of "pradhan pati"

  - **b.** Gender-exclusive quotas in panchayat and ward committees (Kerala's model)

  - **c.** 'Anti-Pradhan Pati' awards

  - **d.** Virtual reality (VR) simulation training to enhance governance skills

### Aspirational Block Program

- **1.** The ABP is modeled on the highly successful Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP) launched in 2018.

- **2.** It was launched by PM in 2023, during the 2nd National Conference of Chief Secretaries

- **3.** Initially covering 500 blocks, the program was expanded in 2025 to 513 blocks across 329 districts in 27 States and 4 Union Territories.

- **4.** It represents a shift from district-level monitoring to more granular, block-level governance.

### Key Features of the Program

- **1.** The 3Cs Strategy: Operates on the pillars of Convergence (of schemes), Collaboration (between Central/State/Local officials), and Competition (Delta rankings).

- **2.** Selection Criteria: Blocks were selected based on a composite index using data from Mission Antyodaya and the Socio-Economic Caste Census (SECC).

- **3.** Thematic Areas & Weightage:

  - **a.** Health & Nutrition (30%)

  - **b.** Education (30%)

  - **c.** Agriculture & Allied Services (20%)

  - **d.** Basic Infrastructure (15%)

  - **e.** Social Development (5%)

- **4.** Progress is tracked via 40 Key Performance Indicators on a real-time digital dashboard.

### Key Achievements

- **1.** Sampoornata Abhiyan Success: In 2024, NITI Aayog launched a 3-month campaign to achieve 100% saturation in 6 specific KPIs in all aspirational blocks.

- **2.** Sampoornata Abhiyan 2.0 was launched in January 2026 (ending April 14, 2026) to sustain the gains and saturate indicators like bovine vaccination and girl-child toilets.

- **3.** Over 4,500 Block Development Officers (BDOs) have been trained as "Leaders of Change" to improve grassroots administrative efficiency.

- **4.** Uses Central Ministry databases for real-time tracking.

- **5.** Over 90% of blocks now have 100% Functional Household Tap Connections (FHTC) under the Jal Jeevan Mission convergence.

- **6.** Financial Inclusion: PMJJBY and PMSBY enrollment moving closer to 100% saturation among eligible beneficiaries in target blocks.

- **7.** Health Coverage: Substantial improvement in TB notification and treatment success rates.

### Major Challenges

- **1.** Financial Resource Gaps: The program relies on convergence of existing funds.

- **2.** Human Resource Shortfall: high vacancy rates for doctors, teachers, and technical staff in remote areas.

- **3.** Data Discrepancies: the quality of data at the "last mile" (village-to-block entry) remains prone to manual errors or over-reporting.

- **4.** Many blocks are in LWE-affected (Left-Wing Extremism) or high-altitude regions, making physical infrastructure delivery slow and costly.

- **5.** One-Size-Fits-All Metrics: Some KPIs (like bovine vaccination) may not be as relevant for urbanized aspirational blocks compared to rural ones.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Move from general KPIs to SWOT-based (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) strategies unique to each block's geography.

- **2.** Provide additional allowances or career incentives for officers serving in aspirational blocks.

- **3.** Introduce a "Block Innovation Fund" to allow BDOs to solve hyper-local problems.

- **4.** Increase the role of SHGs and NGOs in social audits to ensure the benefits of saturation reach the poorest households.

- **5.** Launch public-facing apps like "Mera Block" to allow citizens to track progress and report service delivery gaps in real-time.

### Case Studies

- **1.** Indore Municipal Corp (MP): 7-time winner of Swachh Survekshan; successfully issued India's first Green Municipal Bond for a 60MW solar plant.

- **2.** Ahmedabad (AMC): A pioneer in Land Monetization; appropriated 4.5 sq km for affordable housing using the "Town Planning Scheme" model.

- **3.** Surat (SMC): Achieved 100% sewage water recycling

- **4.** Ambikapur (Chhattisgarh): "Garbage Clinics" - 100% waste segregation through women's SHG model.

- **5.** Bengaluru has implemented strict municipal bylaws requiring large residential complexes to treat and reuse 100% of their greywater

### Performance Analysis

Positive Role of Urban Local Bodies

- **1.** ULBs prepare master plans, regulate land use, and guide sustainable urban growth through infrastructure and policy planning.

- **2.** Promote grassroots democracy through ward committees, public consultations,

- **3.** Provision of Basic Services:

  - **a.** Cities like Indore and Surat have set benchmarks in solid waste management with 100% door-to-door collection and waste segregation.

  - **b.** Puri became the first Indian city to provide 24x7 "drink-from-tap" water to all households.

  - **c.** Surat improved urban mobility with BRTS and smart road infrastructure, reflected in its high Ease of Living scores.

- **4.** Social Inclusion and Empowerment: Reservation for women, SCs, and STs (74th Amendment)

- **5.** Economic Development: Manages local economic activities, marketplaces, and property taxes, contributing to the economic vitality and growth of urban areas.

- **6.** Disaster and Crisis Management: Eg. BMC led COVID-19 management and Mumbai flood response.

- **7.** Improved Urban Livability in Tier-2 Cities - Eg. Shimla, Bhubaneswar, and Kakinada ranked among top cities in the Ease of Living Index 2020 (population < 1 million). Limitations of Urban Local Bodies

- **1.** Funding Challenges

  - **a.** "Fiscal infantilism" - Own revenue of Indian ULBs is less than 1% of GDP, compared to 6-7% in Brazil and South Africa.

  - **b.** Property Tax Inefficiency: Collection efficiency remains low at 35-40%

  - **c.** Post-GST revenue loss: Subsumption of Octroi, sales tax, and entertainment tax reduced ULB income. Eg. BMC lost ₹7,000 crore (~35%) of revenue post-GST.

  - **d.** Weak SFC implementation reduces fiscal predictability.

  - **e.** Most Tier-II cities lack the credit rating required to access private capital markets effectively.
