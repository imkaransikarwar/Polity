## Chapter 3
Citizenship
CITIZENSHIP
In the Indian constitutional framework, Citizenship is governed by Articles 5–11 (Part II) and the Citizenship
Act, 1955. It represents a person's legal status as a member of the sovereign state, entitling them to all civil
and political rights.
Acquisition and Renunciation

#### A. Acquisition (5 Ways)
1. Birth:
a. Every person born in India on or after 26th January, 1950 but before 1st July, 1987.
b. Every person born in India between 1st July, 1987 and 2nd February, 2004 is a citizen of India given
that either of their parents is a citizen of the country at the time of birth.
c. Person born in India on or after 3rd December, 2004 is a citizen if both parents are Indians or at least
one parent is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of birth.
2. Descent: Born outside India to an Indian parent. Must be registered at an Indian consulate within one
year of birth.
3. Registration:
a. Minor children of persons who are citizens of India.
b. A person of Indian origin who is a resident of any country outside undivided India.
c. A person of Indian origin who has been a resident of India for 7 years before applying for
registration.
d. A person who is married to an Indian citizen and is ordinarily resident for 7 years before applying
for registration.
4. Naturalization: For foreigners resident for 12 years (1+11) who meet Third Schedule qualifications
(language, character).
5. Incorporation of Territory: If a new territory becomes part of India (Eg. Pondicherry, 1962), the
Government notifies its citizens.

#### B. Renunciation and Loss (3 Ways)
1. Renunciation: A voluntary act where a citizen of full age makes a declaration. Minor children also lose
citizenship but can resume it at 18.
2. Termination: Automatic loss if a citizen voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country (Single
Citizenship principle).
3. Deprivation: Compulsory termination by the Government if citizenship was obtained by fraud,
disloyalty to the Constitution, or during war with an enemy.
CAA-NRC: Concepts and Evolution
1. CAA (Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019): Amends the 1955 Act to grant expedited citizenship to
persecuted minorities (Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Christians) from Afghanistan,
Bangladesh, and Pakistan who entered India before 31 Dec 2014.
2. NRC (National Register of Citizens): A register of all legal Indian citizens. Mandated by the 2003
Amendment to identify illegal immigrants.

f. In March 2024, the MHA notified the Citizenship Amendment Rules.
Mechanism for Implementing CAA Rules (2024-26)
1. The implementation follows a completely digital, decentralized process bypassing state government
discretion:
2. Applicants must apply via the "Indian Citizenship Online" portal or mobile app with self-declaration of
origin.
3. Relaxed Documentation: Accepts any document showing entry before Dec 31, 2014 (even expired
passports/visas or "locally reputed" certificates).
4. District-Level Committee (DLC): Headed by the Superintendent of Post, this panel verifies documents
and administers the Oath of Allegiance.
5. Empowered Committee (EC): Led by the Director of Census Operations, this central body reviews DLC
recommendations and grants citizenship.
6. Language Test: Applicants must prove proficiency in any language listed in the Eighth Schedule of the
Constitution.
7. Direct Issuance: Citizenship certificates are issued digitally by the EC, removing the previous role of
State District Collectors.
Justification for CAA-NRC
1. Historical Responsibility: India's moral obligation to minorities left in theocratic neighbors after the
1947 Partition.
2. Religious Persecution: Targets specific countries where the state religion (Islam) leads to systemic
persecution of minorities.
3. Reasonable Classification: Article 14 allows "reasonable classification" for a specific group (persecuted
minorities) to achieve a legislative goal.
4. National Security: NRC helps identify illegal infiltrators who may pose a threat to internal security
5. Demographic Protection: Protects the cultural and linguistic identity of indigenous populations,
especially in Assam and Tripura.
6. Resource Management: Ensures that welfare benefits and state resources are reserved for genuine
citizens, not illegal migrants.
7. Humanitarian Relief: Provides a "life of dignity" and legal identity to 31,313 documented refugees
8. Regularizing Migrants: CAA grants retrospective citizenship, closing pending legal cases against those
who fled persecution before 2014.
9. Civilizational Ethos: Aligns with India's history as a "natural home" for those fleeing religious
oppression. Eg. Parsis, Jews.
10. Legal Clarity: Distinguishes between "refugees" (fleeing persecution) and "infiltrators" (economic
migrants), streamlining the naturalization process.

Criticism of CAA-NRC
1. Religious Discrimination: Critics argue CAA violates Article 14 by using religion as a criterion for
citizenship.
2. Exclusion of Muslims: Excludes persecuted Muslim sects like Ahmadiyyas (Pakistan) or Hazaras
(Afghanistan) from the fast-track process.
3. Regional Refugees: Fails to address non-Muslim neighbors like Sri Lanka (Tamils) or Myanmar
(Rohingyas).
4. Assam Accord Violation: CAA's 2014 cut-off contradicts the Assam Accord's 1971 cut-off, causing fears of
demographic shift.
5. NRC Documentation Burden: Millions of poor citizens lack birth certificates; Assam NRC (2019)
excluded 19 lakh people due to paperwork.
6. Statelessness Risk: Fears that a pan-India NRC could render marginalized Muslims stateless if they
cannot prove ancestry.
7. Secularism at Stake: Critics view religion-based citizenship as a threat to the Basic Structure of the
Indian Constitution.
8. Diplomatic Friction: Has caused intermittent strain in ties with Bangladesh and Afghanistan over
"persecution" narratives.
9. Arbitrary Cut-off: The 31 Dec 2014 date is criticized as lacking a logical or scientific basis for
determining persecution.
10. Financial Cost: The Assam NRC cost ₹1,220 crore; a pan-India exercise is estimated to cost billions
without guaranteed accuracy.
Supreme Court Judgments
1. Sarbananda Sonowal v. UOI (2005): Struck down the IMDT Act, calling illegal migration from
Bangladesh an "external aggression" against Assam.
2. Assam Sanmilita Mahasangha (2024): Upheld Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, validating the 1971
cut-off specifically for the state of Assam.
Way Forward
1. Inclusive Amendment: The government could consider adding "persecuted sects" within Islam to make
the law religion-neutral and legally robust.
2. Using the NPR (National Population Register) data to simplify citizenship verification without requiring
50-year-old paper records.
3. India must sign a formal repatriation treaty with Bangladesh to handle those identified as foreigners by
the NRC.
4. Transparency in NRC: Establish clear, lenient evidentiary standards for the poor and illiterate to prevent
accidental mass statelessness.
5. Enacting a comprehensive National Asylum Law to provide a transparent framework for all refugees,
regardless of country or religion.
6. Strengthening Foreigners Tribunals with judicial oversight to ensure fair appeals for those excluded
from citizenship registers.
