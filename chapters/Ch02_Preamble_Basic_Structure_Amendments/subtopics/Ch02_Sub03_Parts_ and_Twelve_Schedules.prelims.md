KEY CONCEPTS
Constitution and Constitutional Government [PRELIMS 2023,2021,2020,2014]
1. A Constitution is the fundamental law that constitutes the organs of government and allocates power among them, but
its chief purpose is to define and limit the powers of government.
2. Constitutional government therefore means limited government - a government bound by the terms of the
Constitution.
3. It places effective restrictions on the authority of the State in the interest of individual liberty, not restrictions on liberty
in the interest of State authority.
4. The devices of limitation in India:
(a) Justiciable Fundamental Rights (Part III), enforceable under Arts 32 and 226.
(b) Judicial review.
(c) Federal division of powers under the Seventh Schedule.
(d) An independent judiciary.
(e) The Basic Structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
5. A government may be popular, elected or multi-party and still not be constitutional if it is not legally limited.

Due Process of Law vs Procedure Established by Law [PRELIMS 2023]
1. Article 21 as drafted adopted the 'procedure established by law' formula (traced to the Japanese Constitution): the
court asks only whether a competent legislature validly enacted the law and whether its procedure was followed.
2. 'Due process of law' (US 5th and 14th Amendments) goes further and requires the law and its procedure to be fair, just
and reasonable - in essence it embodies the principles of natural justice (nemo judex in causa sua - no one a judge in his
own cause; audi alteram partem - hear the other side).
3. In A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras (1950) the Supreme Court read Art 21 literally;
4. In Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978) it held that the procedure under Art 21 must be 'right, just and fair and not
arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive', effectively reading due process into the Constitution.
State, Government, Democracy and Bureaucracy [PRELIMS 2021,2020,2017]
1. In political theory the State has four essential elements - population, a definite territory, a government, and
sovereignty (independence of external control). Hence the correct definition is a community of persons permanently
occupying a definite territory, independent of external control and possessing an organised government;
2. long residence, common culture or tradition make a nation or a society, not a State.
3. The State is permanent and wider;
4. The government is only its agency and changes.
5. (Note: Art 12 gives a separate and wider working definition of 'the State' for the purposes of Part III.) Democracy's
superior virtue is that it calls into activity the intelligence and character of ordinary men and women, resting on
participation, accountability and universal adult franchise rather than on a single dynamic leader.
6. The bureaucracy is the permanent, politically neutral civil service whose characteristic function is the implementation of
public policy framed by the political executive;
7. Art 311 safeguards and the All India Services underpin it.
Rule of Law and Separation of Powers [PRELIMS 2021,2018]
1. Rule of Law comes from Britain and was explained by A.V. Dicey. It has three parts:
(a) Supremacy of law: no arbitrary power; the powers of government are limited.
(b) Equality before law: embodied in Art 14.
(c) Predominance of legal spirit: ordinary courts protect liberty and civil rights.
2. It does not include any 'responsibility of the people to the government';
3. The direction of accountability runs the other way.
4. The Rule of Law is part of the Basic Structure (Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, 1975).
5. Separation of powers - the organic and functional distribution of authority among the legislature, executive and judiciary
with checks and balances - is regarded as the best safeguard of liberty in a liberal democracy because it prevents
concentration of power;
6. India follows it functionally rather than absolutely (Art 50 separates the judiciary from the executive), and it too is part of
the Basic Structure.
Citizenship (Part II, Articles 5-11) [PRELIMS 2021]
1. India has single citizenship and a single domicile - there is no separate State citizenship, unlike the USA.
2. Arts 5-11 (Part II) deal only with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution;
3. Art 11 empowers Parliament to regulate citizenship by law, under which the Citizenship Act, 1955 was enacted.
4. It provides five modes of acquisition - birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and incorporation of territory - and
three modes of loss - renunciation, termination and deprivation.
5. The Central Government can deprive a registered or naturalised citizen of citizenship if the person:
(a) obtained it by fraud;
(b) showed disloyalty to the Constitution;
(c) traded or communicated with the enemy during a war;
(d) was sentenced to two years' imprisonment within five years of registration or naturalisation;
(e) lived outside India for seven years continuously.
So citizenship, once granted, is not indefeasible.

6. The President need only be a citizen of India qualified for election to the Lok Sabha (Art 58) - citizenship by birth is not
required, unlike the US requirement of a natural-born citizen.
Liberty, Equality, Rights and Social Capital [PRELIMS 2019,2018,2017]
1. Liberty is not mere absence of restraint or the opportunity to do whatever one likes;
2. in its positive sense it is the opportunity to develop oneself fully.
3. Law and liberty are complementary, not opposed - 'where there is no law there is no liberty' (John Locke) - because law
restrains interference by others and creates the conditions of freedom;
4. more laws do not automatically mean less liberty.
5. Equality implies primarily the absence of special privileges and equality of opportunity;
6. It does not mean absence of restraint, competition or ideology.
7. Rights are claims of the citizen against the State, recognised and enforced by it - not claims of the State against citizens
nor privileges of a few;
8. in India they are made justiciable through Part III read with Arts 32 and 226.
9. Social capital is a distinct, intangible resource - the level of mutual trust, harmony, networks and reciprocity in a society
- as opposed to physical capital (buildings, infrastructure, machines) and human capital (literacy, skills, working-age
population).
Constitutionalism and Constitutional Morality
1. Constitutionalism is the doctrine that governmental power must be limited by and exercised through law - 'limited
government'.
2. A country may possess a Constitution without having constitutionalism (authoritarian regimes have written
constitutions but unchecked power);
3. the two are therefore not the same thing.
4. Its essential ingredients are: rule of law, separation of powers with checks and balances, judicial review, enforceable
fundamental rights, an independent judiciary, free and fair periodic elections, and a government accountable to the
people.
5. Constitutional morality: a phrase Ambedkar borrowed from the historian George Grote and pressed in the Constituent
Assembly (4 November 1948).
It means following the spirit and core values of the Constitution, not mere literal legality, and the willingness of all actors
to work it with restraint.
6. Illustrations: Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) - the proclamation dissolving the Bihar Assembly on the
Governor's report was held unconstitutional and mala fide;
7. I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) - judicial review is part of the Basic Structure, so even Ninth Schedule laws
inserted after 24 April 1973 can be tested;
8. Navtej Singh Johar (2018) - constitutional morality prevails over popular/social morality.
Sovereignty and its Kinds
Sovereignty is the supreme, original and independent authority of the State, one of the four essential elements of a State
and the one that distinguishes a State from every other association.
KINDS OF SOVEREIGNTY
1. Internal: supremacy over all persons and associations within the territory.
2. External: freedom from foreign control in the conduct of relations with other States.
3. Legal: the authority that makes law and whose commands the courts enforce. In India the Constitution is supreme; in
Britain, Parliament.
4. Political: the electorate behind the legal sovereign, whose will ultimately prevails.
5. Popular: power flows from the people, declared in the opening words WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA and worked through
universal adult franchise.
6. De jure against de facto: legal title to rule as against actual effective control.
7. Pluralist view (Harold Laski): the State is only one association among many and not absolutely supreme.
- Parliamentary sovereignty in the United Kingdom means no court can question an Act of Parliament.
- India instead follows constitutional supremacy, so Parliament's power to legislate and to amend is limited and judicially
reviewable.

Doctrines - I: Severability, Eclipse, Colourable Legislation
1. Doctrine of Severability (Separability) - under Art 13, when only a part of a law offends the Constitution, the court
strikes down only that offending part if it can be separated from the rest;
2. If the valid and invalid parts are inseparable, the whole law falls (A.K. Gopalan, 1950, where only s.14 of the Preventive
Detention Act was voided; R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla, 1957).
3. Doctrine of Eclipse - a pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is not dead but dormant;
4. The dormant law stays valid for past transactions and for people who cannot claim that right (for example, those who
are not citizens, for Art 19 rights).
When an amendment removes the conflict, it revives automatically (Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P., 1955).
5. It does not apply the same way to post-Constitution laws violating Fundamental Rights, which are void from inception
(Deep Chand, 1959;
6. Mahendra Lal Jaini, 1963), though such a law may still operate against non-citizens where the right is available only to
citizens.
7. Doctrine of Colourable Legislation - 'what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly'.
8. a legislature cannot use a subject within its competence as a mere pretence to legislate on a subject outside it.
9. The test goes to legislative competence, not to the motive or bona fides of the legislature (K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v.
State of Orissa, 1953).
Doctrines - II: Pith and Substance, Harmonious Construction, Territorial Nexus, Repugnancy
1. Pith and Substance - to test a law against the Seventh Schedule the court looks at its true nature and character, not its
form;
2. If in pith and substance it falls within the legislature's own List, an incidental encroachment on another List does not
invalidate it (Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee, 1947, Privy Council; F.N. Balsara, 1951).
3. Harmonious Construction - overlapping provisions or List entries must be read so as to give effect to both, and one
should not be so construed as to render the other nugatory;
4. It is used to reconcile Fundamental Rights with DPSPs (In re Kerala Education Bill, 1957; Minerva Mills, 1980).
5. Doctrine of Territorial Nexus: under Art 245(1), a State law normally applies only within that State.
A State law that operates outside the State is still valid if there is a real (not illusory) connection between the State and
the object taxed or regulated (State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala, 1957);
6. Art 245(2) protects Parliament's laws absolutely - no law of Parliament is invalid merely because it operates
extra-territorially.
7. Doctrine of Repugnancy - Art 254(1): on a Concurrent List subject, if a State law is repugnant to a Parliamentary law or
an existing law, the Union law prevails and the State law is void to the extent of repugnancy;
8. Art 254(2): but if the State law was reserved for and received the President's assent, it prevails in that State - subject
to Parliament's power to later add to, amend or repeal it.
TERMS WHERE THE CONSTITUTION IS NOT CLEAR
- Not Mentioned - Federation, Judicial Review, Vice President removal, Offices of leader of house and leader of opposition
- Mentioned but not defined - Minority, Martial law, Untouchability, Cabinet - Added by 44th CAA but without defining
