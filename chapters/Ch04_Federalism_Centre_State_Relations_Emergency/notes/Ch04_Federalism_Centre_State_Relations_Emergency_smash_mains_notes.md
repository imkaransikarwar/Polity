### Federalism

"Indian Union is a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features." - K.C. Wheare. "India is a Union of States, not a federation of states... the Union is indestructible, though the states are not." - B.R. Ambedkar

> "Centre and States are co-equal in their respective fields, bound together by a spirit of cooperation." - SC in Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)

> "Indian federalism is highly centralized, designed more to maintain unity than to promote autonomy." - Paul Brass Federalism is a system of governance where power is distributed between a central authority and regional entities like states or provinces. Scholarly Views on Indian Federalism

- K.C. Wheare - Described Indian federalism as "quasi-federal", meaning a federation with a strong centralizing tendency.

- Granville Austin - a "cooperative federalism" model, where Centre and States are bound in a "seamless web" to achieve socio-economic revolution.

- Ivor Jennings - Stated that India is "a federation with a strong centralising tendency", highlighting the dominant role of the Union.

- D.D. Basu - Termed Indian federalism as a "sui generis model" (unique in itself), combining both federal and unitary features.

- Rajeev Bhargava - Saw Indian federalism as "multi-layered federalism", evolving towards cooperative and competitive models under economic liberalisation.

- Arend Lijphart - Argued that Indian federalism is an example of "consociational democracy", managing pluralism through power-sharing.

- R. Kothari - Viewed Indian federalism as "centripetal federalism", ensuring stability through a strong Centre while granting States participatory space.

### Key Features of Federalism

- **1.** Division of Powers - Powers divided through the Union List, State List, and Concurrent List (Seventh Schedule).

- **2.** Dual Polity - Division of governance between the Union and States, each with its own government.

- **3.** Written Constitution - Provides clarity and certainty in the division of powers.

- **4.** Supremacy of the Constitution - Both Centre and States are bound by the Constitution as the supreme law.

- **5.** Rigid Constitution - Certain provisions can only be amended by special procedure, requiring consent of States.

- **6.** Independent Judiciary - The Supreme Court acts as guardian and interpreter of the Constitution to resolve Centre-State disputes.

- **7.** Bicameralism at Centre - Rajya Sabha represents States in the Union legislature.

- **8.** Financial Autonomy - Distribution of financial resources and functions through Finance Commission and constitutional provisions.

- **9.** Authority of States in Normal Times - States have autonomy in matters under the State List, subject to constitutional limits.

- **10.** Unity with Diversity - Indian federalism accommodates linguistic, cultural, and regional diversities while maintaining national unity. Eg. Article 371

### Unitary Features of the Indian Constitution

- **1.** States are Not Indestructible (Article 3) - The Parliament has the authority to create, merge, or alter state boundaries and names without requiring state consent.

- **2.** Emergency Provisions -

  - **a.** National Emergency (Article 352) - The President can assume extraordinary powers in case of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion.

  - **b.** President's Rule (Article 356) - If a state government fails to function per constitutional provisions, the President can assume direct control, making the federal structure temporarily unitary.

- **3.** Strong Union Legislature -

  - **a.** Residuary Powers (Article 248) - Parliament holds exclusive authority over subjects not listed in the Union, State, or Concurrent lists.

  - **b.** State List Override (Article 249) - The Rajya Sabha can authorize Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the national interest.

- **4.** Law Supremacy (Article 254) - In case of a conflict between state and central laws on a Concurrent List subject, the central law prevails.

- **5.** Strong Union Executive (Article 256) - State governments must comply with laws made by Parliament, and the Union can direct state executives to enforce them.

- **6.** Single Citizenship - Unlike other federations, India provides for only one citizenship, ensuring uniform rights and duties for all citizens across states.

- **7.** All-India Services - The IAS, IPS, and IFS are centrally controlled services that operate in states, strengthening the Union's role in state administration.

- **8.** Integrated Judiciary - India follows a single judicial system with the Supreme Court at the apex, ensuring uniform interpretation of laws across the country.

### Significance of Federalism - PIES Framework

Political Significance

- **1.** Unity in Diversity - Eg. Reorganisation of states on linguistic lines (1956).

- **2.** Checks and Balances - Prevents excessive centralisation. Eg. 7th schedule

- **3.** Adaptability to Regional Aspirations: Eg. The formation of Telangana in 2014 to accommodate regional demands for administrative efficiency and cultural identity. Institutional Significance

- **1.** Conflict Resolution - Constitutional mechanisms like Inter-State Council and Supreme Court adjudication.

- **2.** Rajya Sabha Role - Provides States a voice in national policymaking.

- **3.** Cooperative Federalism - Institutions like the GST Council ensure joint decision-making. Economic Significance

- **1.** Balanced Development - Allows States to pursue region-specific growth strategies. Eg. Gujarat investor's summit

- **2.** Fiscal Federalism - Finance Commission ensures equitable resource devolution (Eg. 16th FC kept states' share to 41%).

- **3.** Policy Innovation - States act as laboratories of democracy; Eg. Midday Meal Scheme in Tamil Nadu later scaled nationally.

### Asymmetric Federalism

Asymmetric federalism refers to an unequal distribution of powers and relationships in the political, administrative, and fiscal spheres among federal units within a federation. According to Riker (1975), federalism is an outcome of rational bargain among various constituents.

### Key Aspects

- **1.** Dual Asymmetry - It exists in both vertical (Centre vs. States) and horizontal (among States/UTs) dimensions.

- **2.** Self-Rule & Shared Rule - India follows a "salad bowl" approach, recognizing distinct cultural identities and permitting self-governance within a unified structure.

- **3.** Founding Vision allows for regional autonomy while ensuring a strong central authority when needed.

### Vertical Asymmetry (Between Centre and States)

- **1.** Article 3 -Parliament can alter state boundaries and names, without state consent

- **2.** Single Citizenship - Unlike US, India has only one citizenship for uniform national identity

- **3.** Articles 352 & 356 empower the Centre to take direct control of states during Emergency.

- **4.** Article 248 - Residuary legislative powers rest solely with the Parliament.

- **5.** Governor Appointment - appointed by the President & serves as the Centre's representative

- **6.** 7th Schedule - On subjects in the Concurrent List, Parliament laws override state laws.

- **7.** All India Services: ultimate control lies with the centre.

### Horizontal Asymmetry (Among States/UTs)

- **1.** Articles 371A-371J - Provide special provisions and autonomy to certain states.

- **2.** Schedule 5 & 6 - self-governance in tribal regions.

- **3.** Article 370 (Revoked in 2019) - special status to J&K with substantial autonomy

- **4.** Unequal representation in the Rajya Sabha: The allocation of seats based on population.

### Fiscal Asymmetry

- **1.** Centre controls buoyant tax sources (income tax, corporate tax, GST share). States bear higher expenditure responsibilities (health, education, welfare, agriculture).

- **2.** Horizontal Devolution - criteria of income disparity, demographic trends, and ecological performance.

- **3.** Article 293: States' borrowing is subject to Centre's consent.

- **4.** CSS - Central share of 90% for Special Category States (60% for others)

- **5.** Special Category Status - Granted by the National Development Council (NDC) to states with hilly terrain, low population density, or economic backwardness.

### Need and Significance

- **1.** Political Significance

  - **a.** Rekha Saxena describes Indian Asymmetric Federalism as 'Self-rule within shared rule'

  - **b.** National Integration - tailored autonomy for preventing alienation and separatism. Eg. Article 371F for Sikkim's accession (1975).

  - **c.** Conflict Resolution - Eg. Mizoram Accord (1986) under Sixth Schedule.

- **2.** Institutional Significance

  - **a.** Decentralised Governance Eg. Autonomous District Councils under Sixth Schedule

  - **b.** Democratic Representation - Eg. Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC, 2020) for STs.

- **3.** Economic Significance

  - **a.** Bridging Regional Imbalances - Eg. N-E states get 90:10 funding ratio under CSS.

  - **b.** Equity in resource distribution - Hill states like Himachal, UK get higher devolution.

- **4.** Social Significance

  - **a.** Cultural Autonomy - Eg. Nagaland's customary laws protected under Article 371A

  - **c.** Unity in Diversity - Builds confidence in communities within a national framework. Eg. Goa retained Portuguese Civil Code post-integration.

- **2.** Institutional

  - **a.** Complexity in Governance - Eg. Erstwhile J&K had separate civil and criminal procedure, no reservations for SC/STs

  - **a.** Uneven Development - Fiscal and institutional asymmetries deepen regional inequalities. Eg. NE states dependent on central grants

- **4.** Social

### Cooperative Federalism

Cooperative federalism refers to a governance model where national, state, and local governments work collectively to address common challenges through collaboration and shared responsibilities. The SC, in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977), affirmed that India's federalism is predominantly cooperative, requiring mutual interaction between Centre and States.

### Competitive Federalism

Competitive federalism refers to the healthy competition among different levels of government within a federal system to enhance efficiency, governance, and resource attraction.

### Steps Taken Towards Competitive Federalism

- **1.** Monthly rankings of 112 Aspirational Districts create "fame and shame" pressure on district collectors to improve social indicators.

- **2.** Ease of Doing Business (EoDB) Rankings - DPIIT ranks states based on their implementation of the Business Reform Action Plan (BRAP).

- **3.** Fiscal incentives - 16th FC has linked a portion of central grants to state performance in areas like fiscal discipline, forest conservation, and implementation of power sector reforms.

- **4.** Sectoral Performance Indices

  - **a.** Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) India Index: Ranks states on 16 goals, pushing them to achieve universal targets like poverty reduction and clean energy.

  - **b.** India Innovation Index: Measures the innovation ecosystem across states, encouraging a "knowledge economy."

  - **c.** Export Preparedness Index (EPI): Identifies the export potential and performance of states, pushing landlocked and coastal states to build better logistics.

  - **d.** State Health Index: Compares health outcomes, driving states like Uttar Pradesh and Bihar to bridge gaps with leaders like Kerala.

  - **e.** Composite Water Management Index - Measuring progress on water resource management.

- **5.** Reforms in Labor Laws and Land Acquisition - States have reformed labor laws and land acquisition processes to attract investment

- **6.** PM Gati Shakti: States compete to align their industrial corridors and logistic parks with the national master plan to receive central funding and attract multinational manufacturing hubs

- **7.** Competitive Social Welfare Models - Eg. "Delhi Education Model" & "Odisha 5T Transformation" has forced other states to rethink school infrastructure and digital governance to stay politically and economically competitive.

### Significance of Competitive Federalism

- **1.** Investment Attraction: Eg. labor reforms and land subsidies to attract subsidies

- **2.** Efficiency in Service Delivery: Public rankings create reputational pressure on governments to improve basic services. Eg. Jal Jeevan Mission dashboard.

- **3.** Innovation in Public Policy: States act as "policy laboratories," testing unique welfare models that are later scaled nationally. Eg. Odisha's 5T school transformation model (2025)

- **4.** Aspirational Motivation: Creates a "race to the top" among backward districts, moving them toward national development averages

- **5.** Global Competitiveness: Helps India climb global indices like the Global Innovation Index by improving sub-national performance.

- **6.** Fiscal Discipline: Performance-linked grants from the 16th Finance Commission incentivize states to maintain low fiscal deficits.

- **7.** Accountability to Citizens: Regular ranking releases allow voters to compare their state's progress with neighbors, increasing political accountability.

### Challenges in Competitive Federalism

- **1.** Regional Inequality: Resource-rich southern and western states attract more FDI than "BIMARU" states. Eg. five states account for nearly 70% of India's total FDI equity inflows.

- **2.** North-South Divide over the distribution of central taxes and political representation. Eg. southern state's protest against population-based criteria under 16th FC.

- **3.** Race to the Bottom: States may aggressively cut corporate taxes or dilute labor protections to underbid their neighbors.

- **4.** Inter-State Trust Deficit: High-intensity competition for projects (like semiconductor fabs) can sour cooperative relations between neighboring states.

- **5.** One-Size-Fits-All Indices: Rankings often use uniform parameters that ignore the unique geographical or historical constraints of hill/tribal states.

- **6.** Data Manipulation Risks: High pressure to rank well can lead states to report "window-dressed" data to central agencies.

- **7.** Limited Revenue Autonomy: Post-GST, states have limited tax-raising powers, making it hard to fund the infrastructure needed to compete.

- **8.** Political Bias Allegations: Opposition-ruled states often allege that central rankings and fund allocations are politically motivated or biased.

### Fiscal Federalism

It entails the division of responsibilities and resources between the central and state governments, with the Finance Commission playing a key role in recommending tax proceeds distribution.

### Constitutional Provisions

Seventh Schedule (Article 246)

- **1.** Union List: Income Tax, Customs, and Excise on tobacco/petroleum

- **2.** State List: Land Revenue, Agricultural Income Tax, Excise on alcoholic liquors, and Professional Tax.

- **3.** Concurrent List: After the 101st Amendment (2016), both Centre and States have concurrent powers to levy Goods and Services Tax (GST) under Article 246A. Revenue Sharing Mechanisms

- **1.** Article 268: Duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by the States (Eg. Stamp duties).

- **2.** Article 269: Taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned to the States (Eg. tax on inter-state trade).

- **3.** Article 269A: Specifically governs the levy and collection of IGST (Integrated GST) on inter-state trade, which is shared between the Union and States.

- **4.** Article 270: The "Divisible Pool." Most taxes levied by the Union are distributed between the Union and States based on Finance Commission recommendations.

- **5.** Article 271: Power of the Union to levy surcharges and cesses for its own purposes. Notably, these are not shared with the States. Institutional Framework

- **1.** Article 280 (FC): A quasi-judicial body constituted by the President every five years to recommend the vertical and horizontal distribution of net tax proceeds.

- **2.** Article 279A (GST Council) highest decision-making body for GST rates, exemptions, and thresholds. Grants-in-Aid

- **1.** Article 275 (Statutory Grants): Mandatory grants paid out of the Consolidated Fund of India to States in need

- **2.** Article 282 (Discretionary Grants): Allows Union and States to make grants for any "public purpose," even if the subject is not within their respective legislative lists Borrowing Powers

- **1.** Article 292: Governs the borrowing powers of the Central Government.

- **2.** Article 293: Governs the borrowing powers of the State Governments. State must obtain the Centre's consent to raise a new loan if it has any outstanding liabilities to the Union.

### Issues

- **1.** North-South Equity Gap: Southern states feel "penalized" for successful population control under the 2011 Census-based devolution formulas.

- **2.** Uttar Pradesh receives more than the combined share of the five southern states - Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and Kerala.

- **3.** Rise of Non-Devolvable Cesses - Centre's non-shareable revenue grew from 12.8% (2015-20) to 18.5% (2020-24).

- **4.** Vertical Imbalance - States' effective share shrank from 35% (2015-20) to ~31% (2020-24) of Centre's gross tax revenue.

- **5.** Increase in Conditional Transfers - Grants are linked to Union-defined conditions, limiting state policy flexibility. Eg. Jal Jeevan mission grants based on target coverage

- **6.** Borrowing Restrictions - Centre enforces a 3% SDP borrowing cap, limiting states' fiscal flexibility

- **7.** Centrally Sponsored Scheme (CSS) Burden: States must provide 40% matching funds for schemes designed entirely by the Central Government.

- **8.** Erosion of Tax Autonomy: Post-GST, states have lost the power to vary tax rates on most goods to address local emergencies.

- **9.** Discretionary Grant Delays: Central grants under Article 282 are often seen as politically motivated or delayed for opposition-ruled states.

- **10.** Inter-State Council Inactivity: The lack of regular meetings of the Inter-State Council (Article 263) prevents resolution of fiscal disputes through dialogue. Scholar Views

- Upendra Baxi - Warned against "executive federalism", where Centre dictates state policy through fiscal and administrative tools rather than genuine consultation.

- Nidhi Sharma ("Quasi-federal constitution?) - posits that India's avoidance of explicit "federal" vocabulary (preferring "Union of States") has allowed the Union and judiciary to reinforce centralization over time.

- Louis Tillin in "An Incomplete Bargain" (Asian Survey, 2025) - argues that state reorganizations have paradoxically reinforced central dominance while masking persistent federal tensions. Major SC Judgments on Federalism

- State of WB v. UoI (1963): first case to define India's federal character, ruling that the Constitution is not "truly federal" but has a strong central bias for national integrity.

- S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): it declared federalism a "Basic Structure" of the Constitution and made the dismissal of state governments (Article 356) justiciable.

- Kuldip Nayar (2006): Upheld the removal of the "domicile requirement" for RS members, confirming that India maintains a "unitary spirit" in legislative representation

- Madras Bar Association: Union cannot bypass the "federal balance" by creating parallel judicial bodies that strip High Courts of their powers.

- NCT of Delhi: In a federal democracy, the elected government (Delhi) must have control over "services," emphasizing that "real" power must lie with the people's representatives.

- State of Punjab v. Governor: Critiqued Governors sitting on state bills indefinitely, ruling that the Governor cannot "thwart the normal course of lawmaking" in a federal setup.

### Way Forward for federalism

### Office of Governor

> "The Governor is a constitutional head and the custodian of the Constitution within the state." - Ambedkar "He serves as a bridge, ensuring that the dual system of governance works in harmony within the constitutional framework." - S. Radhakrishnan

### Powers and Functions of the Governor

The Governor's role is similar to the President of India, but without diplomatic, military, or emergency powers. Executive Powers

- **1.** Appoints the Chief Minister(Article 164), Ministers, Advocate General(Article 165), State Election Commissioner(Article 243K), and Public Service Commission members(Article 316).

- **2.** Oversees state executive actions and serves as Chancellor of state universities.

- **3.** Can recommend President's Rule(Article 356) and act as the Centre's representative during emergencies. Legislative Powers

- **1.** Summons, prorogues, and addresses the legislature(Article 174).

- **2.** Appoints presiding officers and nominated members(Article 171).

- **3.** Can exercise veto over bills by assenting, withholding, returning, or reserving them for the President(Article 200). Financial Powers

- **1.** Supervises the state budget and approves money bills(Article 199).

- **2.** Can make advances from the Contingency Fund(Article 267).

- **3.** Establishes a State Finance Commission every five years(Article 243I) to assess panchayats and municipalities' financial health. Judicial Powers

- **1.** Grants pardons, reprieves, and commutations (Article 161) for state offenses.

- **2.** Consulted on state high court judge appointments(Article 217).

- **3.** Oversees appointments, postings, and promotions of district and judicial officers(Article 233 and 234) in consultation with the High Court and State Public Service Commission. Case Studies

- Tamil Nadu: SC expressed serious concern over Governor's decision to withhold assent to 10 Bills passed by the state legislature, some pending since 2020. Three days later, all 10 Bills were returned for reconsideration, but no reasons were provided for the decision.

- Kerala: Governor refused to sign an ordinance to transfer the powers of Chancellor of state universities from the Governor to the Chief Minister.

### Issues pertaining to the office of governor

- **1.** Appointment retired politicians,former Bureaucrats and party loyalists, using the post as a political reward.

- **2.** Acting as Centre's Agent - Governors promote ruling party interests. Often seen as a retirement package for politicians.

- **3.** Indefinite Delay of Bills: Governors frequently use "pocket vetoes" to stall state legislation. Eg. Tamil Nadu NEET Bill controversy

- **4.** Misuse of Discretionary Powers: Discretion in government formation during hung assemblies often favors the party ruling at the Centre. Eg. 2019 Maharashtra case

- **5.** Governors are removed when central governments change, as there is no impeachment provision. Eg. in 1989, the V.P Singh government asked all Governors to resign.

- **6.** Article 356 Misuse - Governors recommend President's Rule prematurely, often targeting opposition states.

- **7.** Politicization of Public Addresses: Eg. 2025 Kerala Assembly session saw a constitutional standoff when the Governor skipped key policy paragraphs.

### SC on the position of Governor

- **1.** Shamsher Singh vs. State of Punjab (1974) - Governor must act on the advice of the Council of Ministers, except where the Constitution states otherwise.

- **2.** S.R. Bommai vs. Union of India (1994) - President's Rule is subject to judicial review, and CM must be allowed a floor test.

- **3.** Rameshwar Prasad vs. UoI (2006) - Governor's actions are open to judicial review. Article 361(1) does not grant immunity in cases of mala fide or unconstitutional acts.

- **4.** B.P. Singhal vs. Union of India (2010) - President cannot remove Governors arbitrarily; change in central government is not a valid reason for dismissal.

- **5.** Nabam Rebia (2016) - Governor must follow the Council of Ministers' advice; cannot disqualify the Speaker or summon Assembly unilaterally.

- **6.** State of TN v. Governor of TN - Introduced the "Deemed Assent" doctrine (invoking Article 142). It established that once a bill is "re-passed" by the Assembly, the Governor must sign it.

- **7.** State of Kerala v. Governor - The Court clarified that while it cannot fix a rigid "calendar deadline" for every bill, prolonged and unexplained inaction is justiciable.

### Inter-State Council

The Inter-State Council (ISC) is a Constitutionally mandated body established under Article 263 of the Constitution of India that facilitates coordination and cooperation between the central government and the state governments. It was set up on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission.

### Mandate

- **1.** To enquire and advise upon inter-state disputes.

- **2.** Investigating & discussing subjects in which states or the Centre and the states have a common interest.

- **3.** Recommending on any matter for better coordination of policy & action.

- **4.** Its decisions are advisory and not binding.

### Issues

- **1.** Infrequent Meetings - ISC has only met 11 times since 1990 (last meeting in 2016)

- **2.** Limited Agenda Scope - lacks focus on emerging inter-state disputes and broader governance issues.

- **3.** Non-Binding Recommendations

- **4.** Lack of Functional Autonomy - The absence of an independent professional secretariat limits ISC's operational efficiency

### Way Forward

- **1.** Constitutional Status - Amend Article 263 to make ISC a permanent constitutional body, ensuring regular meetings and enforceable decisions.

- **2.** Enhance Technical Expertise - Include non-partisan experts and specialists to strengthen policy research and dispute resolution (as suggested by Sarkaria & Punchhi Commissions).

- **3.** Establish a Dedicated Secretariat with officials from both the Centre and states for efficient coordination. Eg. Canada's Intergovernmental Conference Secretariat.

- **4.** Expand ISC's Scope - Empower ISC to review and approve Finance Commission terms of reference, ensuring greater state involvement in fiscal matters (NCRWC).

- **5.** Strengthen Dispute Resolution Role - Equip ISC with legal and mediation powers to resolve Centre-State and inter-state conflicts, particularly in water sharing, taxation, and administrative jurisdiction.

### Inter-State Water Disputes

India has 14 major rivers, all of which are inter-state rivers. Despite constitutional and legal provisions, inter-state river disputes remain a persistent challenge to India's federal system. Recently, Ravi and Beas water tribunal (formed in 1986), got an extension of another year.

### Constitutional and Legislative Provisions

- **1.** Article 262 allows Parliament to:

  - **a.** Create laws for resolving disputes over inter-state river waters.

  - **b.** Exclude the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and other courts from such disputes.

- **2.** Seventh Schedule (Distribution of Power):

  - **a.** Entry 17 (State List): Gives states power over water supply, irrigation, canals, drainage, embankments, and water power.

  - **b.** Entry 56 (Union List): Gives the Central Government power over the regulation and development of inter-state rivers and river valleys.

---

### [Smash Mains Source Page 98](../pages/mains/mains_page_098.pdf)

- **3.** Inter-State River Water Disputes (ISRWD) Act, 1956: it allows a state to request the Centre to refer a dispute to a Tribunal. The decision of the Tribunal is final and binding. Once published in the Official Gazette, it has the same force as an order of the Supreme Court.

- **4.** River Boards Act, 1956: Provides for the establishment of boards for the regulation and development of inter-state rivers. However, this Act has remained largely a "dead letter" as no such board has been successfully empowered.

- **5.** The ISRWD (Amendment) Bill: Aimed at streamlining the process by replacing ad-hoc tribunals with a single, permanent "Inter-State River Water Disputes Tribunal" and introducing a Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC) for preliminary mediation.

### Reasons

- **1.** Pre-independence agreements were made without considering future state boundaries. Eg. Mysore Kingdom-Madras Presidency agreements led to the Cauvery dispute.

- **2.** Non-Basin State Boundaries: State reorganization was based on language, not geography.

- **3.** Vague "Equitable Apportionment": There is no fixed mathematical formula for "fair share," leaving water allocation to subjective and lengthy tribunal interpretations.

- **4.** Unilateral Dam Construction: Eg. Karnataka's Mekedatu project proposal opposed by Tamil Nadu over fears of reduced downstream flow.

- **5.** Supreme Court Intervention (Article 136): Despite the bar under Article 262, states frequently use "Special Leave Petitions" to appeal tribunal awards

- **6.** Seventh Schedule conflict: Centre regulates inter-state river disputes, but states control water supply & irrigation, leading to overlapping authority.

- **7.** Article 262 empowers Parliament to form Water Disputes Tribunals, but no time limit leads to prolonged conflicts. Eg. Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal took 17 years to decide.

- **8.** Overuse, pollution, and climate change have intensified disputes as states compete for diminishing resources.

- **9.** Agricultural & industrial demand: Rising irrigation & industry needs exceed available resources, leading to conflicts. Eg. Narmada dispute among Gujarat, MP, Maharashtra.

- **10.** Inconsistent data on river flow, usage, and availability prevents fair water-sharing decisions, leading to implementation challenges.

- **11.** Regional parties use disputes as electoral issues, reducing efforts for a permanent resolution.

- **12.** Lack of political will to implement tribunal and Supreme Court rulings, as governments fear voter backlash.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Leverage inter-state council to develop consensus among states

- **2.** Set time limits for tribunals to deliver awards to prevent prolonged disputes.

- **3.** Restrict appeals by amending laws to limit the scope of Article 136 (Special Leave Petition).

- **4.** Include multidisciplinary experts in hydrology, environmental science, and engineering for informed decision-making.

- **5.** Develop a centralized hydrological database for transparent and reliable water availability data.

- **6.** Utilize remote sensing and GIS technology for accurate monitoring of river flows.

- **7.** Establish regular review mechanisms to assess the implementation of tribunal awards.

### 7th Schedule

Article 246 of the Constitution defines three lists in the Seventh Schedule: Union, State, and Concurrent. Need For Revision Arguments against

- **3.** A strong central role has historically helped in national integration, particularly in security matters.

- **4.** Judicial and Bureaucratic Overload - A major revision would result in increased legal disputes and interpretational challenges for judiciary.

- **2.** Empowering Local Governments - Introducing a Local Government List can strengthen grassroots governance and decentralization.

### President's Rule

President's Rule ensures federal stability during crises and is governed by Articles 355 to 357 (Part XVIII) and Article 365 (Part XIX) of the Indian Constitution.

- **1.** Article 355 - Mandates the Union to protect states from external aggression and internal disturbances.

- **2.** Article 356 - Allows the President to assume state government functions if the constitutional machinery fails.

- **3.** Article 357 - Grants the President power to legislate for a state via ordinance when President's Rule is in effect.

- **4.** Article 365 - Specifies the consequences for a state failing to comply with Union directives, potentially leading to President's Rule.

### Impact of President's Rule

- **1.** State Executive:

- **2.** Assumption of State Government Functions

- **3.** Dismissal of Council of Ministers

- **4.** The President can sanction expenditure from the state consolidated fund when the Lok Sabha is not in session, pending Parliament's approval.

- **5.** Ordinance Promulgation by the President.

- **6.** State Legislature:

- **7.** The President can declare that the powers of the state legislature are to be exercised by the Parliament.

- **8.** Suspension or Dissolution of State Legislative Assembly

- **9.** Parliament's Authority Over State Matters: passing state bills and budgets.

- **10.** Laws enacted by Parliament continue to remain effective even after the imposition of President's Rule.

- **11.** Judiciary: The Constitutional position, status, powers, and functions of the State High Court remain unchanged

- **12.** Fundamental Rights of Citizens: The fundamental Rights of citizens are not impacted upon use of Article 356.

### Issues pertaining to Article 356

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar hoped that the drastic power under Article 356 would remain a Dead Letter and only be a last resort.

- **1.** Arbitrary Dismissal of Governments: Eg. invoked over 100 times since independence

- **2.** Political Misuse - Often invoked to remove opposition-led governments. Eg. President rule in Kerala in 1959.

- **3.** Prolonged Use of President's Rule: Eg. over 4 years in Jammu and Kashmir

- **4.** The term "failure of constitutional machinery" lacks a precise legal definition, allowing for subjective central interpretation.

- **5.** Partisan Role of Governors: Eg. Sarkaria Commission noted that 90% of Article 356 cases were based on purely political gubernatorial recommendations.

- **6.** Bypassing the Floor Test: Eg. In the S.R. Bommai (1994) case, the CM was dismissed despite offering to prove his majority.

- **7.** Negative agenda: Invoked during hung assemblies to prevent certain coalitions from forming, rather than exploring alternative governments.

- **8.** Delayed Judicial Restitutions: Eg. It took five years for the SC to deliver the Bommai judgment, long after the term ended.

### Key Recommendations

- **1.** Supreme Court Guidelines: In the S.R. Bommai case (1994)

  - **a.** Judicial Review: Proclamations of President's Rule are subject to judicial review.

  - **b.** Power under Article 356 is exceptional and should be used only in special situations.

  - **c.** The President's satisfaction must be based on relevant material. The court can assess the relevance but not the correctness or adequacy of the material.

  - **d.** Floor Test: The loss of confidence in the Legislative Assembly should be decided on the floor of the House.

  - **e.** Assembly Suspension: The State Legislative Assembly can only be dissolved after parliamentary approval of the proclamation. Until then, it can be suspended.

- **3.** Reasons for Proclamation: The President's proclamation of President's Rule should state reasons for the decision.

- **4.** Punchhi Commission Recommendations

- **5.** Amendments for Bommai Guidelines: Amend the article to incorporate guidelines from the SC in the Bommai case.

- **6.** Localized Emergency: Introduce a 'Localized Emergency' to address issues at town/district levels without dissolving the state legislature, fulfilling the Union's duty to protect states as per Article 355.

### Financial Emergency (Article 360)

It is a constitutional provision in India that allows the Union government to take control of the financial affairs of the State and the country if the economic stability or credit of India is threatened.

### Proclamation

- **1.** The President can declare a Financial Emergency if he is satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India (or any part of its territory) is threatened.

- **2.** The proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within two months by a simple majority. Once approved, it continues indefinitely until revoked by the President. There is no maximum period prescribed, and it does not require periodic approval like a National Emergency.

### Key Effects

- **1.** Executive Control: The Union can give directions to any State to observe specific canons of "financial propriety."

- **2.** The President can issue orders to reduce the salaries and allowances of any class of persons serving the State or the Union, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.

- **3.** The President can require that all Money Bills or other financial bills passed by a State Legislature be reserved for his consideration.

- **4.** The Union may modify the allocation of taxes and revenue between the Center and States.

### National Emergency (Article 352)

Under Article 352, The President can declare a national emergency when the security of India or a part of it is threatened by war or external aggression or armed rebellion (inserted by 44th amendment). The President can declare a national emergency even before the actual occurrence of war or armed rebellion or external aggression.

### Procedure

- **1.** The President can only proclaim an emergency after receiving a written recommendation from the Union Cabinet (added by 44th amendment).

- **2.** Both Houses must approve the proclamation within one month by a Special Majority (majority of the total membership & 2/3rd of the members present & voting).

- **3.** Once approved, the emergency lasts for six months. It can be extended indefinitely, but it must be re-approved by Parliament every six months.

- **4.** The President can revoke the proclamation at any time via a subsequent proclamation. This does not require parliamentary approval.

- **5.** The President must revoke the emergency if the Lok Sabha passes a resolution disapproving its continuation by a simple majority.

### Key Safeguards under the 44th Amendment (1978)

- **1.** Replaced the vague term "internal disturbance" with "Armed Rebellion" to prevent political misuse of the emergency.

- **2.** Written Cabinet Advice: The President can only proclaim an emergency on the written recommendation of the entire Cabinet, not just the PM.

- **3.** Stricter Approval: Reduced the approval window from two months to one month and mandated a Special Majority.

- **4.** Enhanced Revocation: Empowered the Lok Sabha to force a revocation via a Simple Majority resolution.

- **5.** Inviolable Rights: Prohibited the suspension of Articles 20 (Protection in respect of conviction for offences) and 21 (Protection of life and personal liberty).

### Effects of National Emergency

- **1.** Executive: The Union government can give directions to any State on any matter. State governments are not dismissed, but they come under the total control of the Center.

- **2.** Legislative: Parliament gains the power to make laws on subjects listed in the State List. The president can issue ordinances on State subjects also, if the parliament is not in session.

- **3.** Financial: The President can modify the constitutional distribution of revenues between the Center and the States.

- **4.** Fundamental Rights:

  - **a.** Article 358: Automatically suspends the freedoms guaranteed by Article 19

  - **b.** Article 359: The President can suspend the right to move courts for the enforcement of other rights, EXCEPT Articles 20 & 21

### Justification of National Emergency Provisions

- **1.** Ensuring National Integrity & preventing the disintegration of the country during war.

- **2.** Parliament can make laws on any subject in the State List for coordinated national response.

- **3.** Financial Resource Mobilization: Allows the President to modify the distribution of revenues to prioritize national security over local spending.

- **4.** Transitions the "slow" federal system into a "fast" unitary one to handle crises effectively.

- **5.** Allows the Lok Sabha's term to be extended when holding elections is impossible.

- **6.** Acts as a "safety valve" that allows the state to suspend normal law to save the law itself.

- **7.** Follows the "Necessity Doctrine" recognized in most democracies, from the US (War Powers) to the UK (Emergency Acts).

- **1.** Erosion of Federalism: State governments become subordinates to the Centre.

- **2.** Suspension of Civil Liberties: Article 19 is automatically suspended. Eg. Mass censorship of the press in 1975.

- **3.** Potential for "Constitutional Dictatorship": An ambitious PM could theoretically maintain an emergency indefinitely through a compliant Parliamentary majority.

- **4.** Impact on Judicial Review: While the Minerva Mills (1980) case allows limited review, challenging "Presidential Satisfaction" remains extremely difficult.

- **5.** Economic Centralization can cripple state-level social welfare schemes and local governance.

- **6.** Vagueness of "Armed Rebellion": While better than "internal disturbance," it still lacks a precise legal threshold for "scale."

- **1.** Time-Limited Article 19 Suspension: Ensure Article 19 is only suspended in conflict zones, not the entire country, during partial emergencies.

- **2.** Strengthening Federal Veto: Require a resolution from 1/3rd of State Assemblies to approve an "Internal Emergency" (Armed Rebellion).

- **3.** Mandate a bi-monthly report by the NHRC on the status of detainees during the emergency

- **4.** Use the Inter-State Council as a consultation platform before invoking Article 352 for internal matters.

### Presidential Reference

Recently, The President invoked Article 143(1) of the Constitution to seek the Supreme Court's opinion on the scope of powers of Governors and the President under Articles 200 and 201. The reference follows a recent Supreme Court ruling that set clear timelines for Governors and the President to act on bills passed by state legislatures. The verdict also introduced the idea of 'deemed consent' to address delays caused by gubernatorial inaction. It is a power that the President has invoked on at least 15 occasions since 1950. In 1951, India's first President Rajendra Prasad made the first reference to the Supreme Court in relation to provisions of Delhi Laws Act, 1912, the Ajmer-Merwara (Extension of Laws) Act, 1947 and Part C of the States (Laws) Act of 1950.

### Article 143 (Advisory jurisdiction of SC)

Article 143(1) - Discretionary Reference

- **1.** The President may refer to the Supreme Court a question of law or fact that is of public importance.

- **2.** The Supreme Court may choose to answer the reference or not.

- **3.** The Court's opinion is advisory, not binding, but carries significant constitutional weight. Article 143(2) - Mandatory Reference

- **1.** If any question arises relating to pre-constitutional treaties or agreements, the President must refer it to the Court.

- **2.** The Court is obligated to give its opinion in this case. Article 145(3) requires any such reference to be heard by five judges, after which the SC returns the reference to the President with the majority opinion. Important Judgments

- In re Kerala Education Bill (1958) - First use of Article 143(1). Set precedent for judicial restraint and clarified that advisory opinion is not binding.

### Significance of Article 143

- **1.** Under the Constitution, the President acts on the aid and advice of the Cabinet. The advisory jurisdiction allows her the means to seek independent advice on constitutional matters.

- **2.** Bridge Between Executive and Judiciary - consultation with the Supreme Court strengthens coordination and clarity between constitutional organs.

- **3.** Clarifies Constitutional Ambiguities - Helps in interpreting grey areas of the Constitution, laws, or treaties.

- **4.** Prevents Legal Disputes - Advisory opinions can pre-empt future litigation by clarifying constitutional questions before conflicts arise.

- **5.** Democratic and Transparent Governance - Encourages legal scrutiny of proposed policies or actions without adversarial litigation.

- **6.** Non-Binding Yet Influential - Though not binding, SC's opinion carries great moral and constitutional authority, guiding executive and legislative decisions.

- **7.** Used in Landmark Cases - Played a key role in Berubari case (1960), Special Courts Bill (1979), and Cauvery dispute (1992) - shaping constitutional jurisprudence.

### Major Issues

- **1.** Non-Binding Nature of Opinion

- **2.** Discretion to Decline Reference - Eg. In the Ram Janmabhoomi reference (1993), the Court declined to answer, citing lack of a legal issue.

- **3.** Possibility of Political Misuse - Executive may use Article 143 to politically deflect controversial issues or influence ongoing public debates.

- **4.** Risks converting the Court into a consultative body for the executive.

- **5.** Lack of Procedural Guidelines - The Constitution does not specify detailed procedures or timelines for the Court to respond to a reference.

- **6.** Overlaps with Judicial Review - Advisory opinions under Article 143 may influence or pre-judge issues that could later come before the Court in a judicial capacity.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Make Advisory Opinion Binding in Limited Cases, such as, Constitutional interpretation, Federal disputes, Emergency powers.

- **2.** Introduce Time-Bound Procedure for the Supreme Court

- **3.** Establish clear guidelines (either through statute or SC rules) on: What constitutes a valid "question of public importance" and When the Court may decline a reference.

- **4.** Publish Detailed Reasons for Refusal

- **5.** Require the government to table an action taken report in Parliament on Court's opinion.
