- **3.** Inter-State River Water Disputes (ISRWD) Act, 1956: it allows a state to request the Centre to refer a dispute to a Tribunal. The decision of the Tribunal is final and binding. Once published in the Official Gazette, it has the same force as an order of the Supreme Court.

- **4.** River Boards Act, 1956: Provides for the establishment of boards for the regulation and development of inter-state rivers. However, this Act has remained largely a "dead letter" as no such board has been successfully empowered.

- **5.** The ISRWD (Amendment) Bill: Aimed at streamlining the process by replacing ad-hoc tribunals with a single, permanent "Inter-State River Water Disputes Tribunal" and introducing a Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC) for preliminary mediation.

### Reasons

- **1.** Pre-independence agreements were made without considering future state boundaries. Eg. Mysore Kingdom-Madras Presidency agreements led to the Cauvery dispute.

- **2.** Non-Basin State Boundaries: State reorganization was based on language, not geography.

- **3.** Vague "Equitable Apportionment": There is no fixed mathematical formula for "fair share," leaving water allocation to subjective and lengthy tribunal interpretations.

- **4.** Unilateral Dam Construction: Eg. Karnataka's Mekedatu project proposal opposed by Tamil Nadu over fears of reduced downstream flow.

- **5.** Supreme Court Intervention (Article 136): Despite the bar under Article 262, states frequently use "Special Leave Petitions" to appeal tribunal awards

- **6.** Seventh Schedule conflict: Centre regulates inter-state river disputes, but states control water supply & irrigation, leading to overlapping authority.

- **7.** Article 262 empowers Parliament to form Water Disputes Tribunals, but no time limit leads to prolonged conflicts. Eg. Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal took 17 years to decide.

- **8.** Overuse, pollution, and climate change have intensified disputes as states compete for diminishing resources.

- **9.** Agricultural & industrial demand: Rising irrigation & industry needs exceed available resources, leading to conflicts. Eg. Narmada dispute among Gujarat, MP, Maharashtra.

- **10.** Inconsistent data on river flow, usage, and availability prevents fair water-sharing decisions, leading to implementation challenges.

- **11.** Regional parties use disputes as electoral issues, reducing efforts for a permanent resolution.

- **12.** Lack of political will to implement tribunal and Supreme Court rulings, as governments fear voter backlash.

### Way Forward

- **1.** Leverage inter-state council to develop consensus among states

- **2.** Set time limits for tribunals to deliver awards to prevent prolonged disputes.

- **3.** Restrict appeals by amending laws to limit the scope of Article 136 (Special Leave Petition).

- **4.** Include multidisciplinary experts in hydrology, environmental science, and engineering for informed decision-making.

- **5.** Develop a centralized hydrological database for transparent and reliable water availability data.

- **6.** Utilize remote sensing and GIS technology for accurate monitoring of river flows.

- **7.** Establish regular review mechanisms to assess the implementation of tribunal awards.
