Federalism
"Indian Union is a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary
unitary features." - K.C. Wheare.
"India is a Union of States, not a federation of states... the Union is indestructible, though the states are not." -
B.R. Ambedkar
"Centre and States are co-equal in their respective fields, bound together by a spirit of cooperation." - SC in
Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)
"Indian federalism is highly centralized, designed more to maintain unity than to promote autonomy." - Paul
Brass
Federalism is a system of governance where power is distributed between a central authority and regional
entities like states or provinces.
Scholarly Views on Indian Federalism
- K.C. Wheare - Described Indian federalism as "quasi-federal", meaning a federation with a strong
centralizing tendency.
- Granville Austin - a "cooperative federalism" model, where Centre and States are bound in a "seamless
web" to achieve socio-economic revolution.
- Ivor Jennings - Stated that India is "a federation with a strong centralising tendency", highlighting
the dominant role of the Union.
- D.D. Basu - Termed Indian federalism as a "sui generis model" (unique in itself), combining both
federal and unitary features.
- Rajeev Bhargava - Saw Indian federalism as "multi-layered federalism", evolving towards cooperative
and competitive models under economic liberalisation.
- Arend Lijphart - Argued that Indian federalism is an example of "consociational democracy",
managing pluralism through power-sharing.
- R. Kothari - Viewed Indian federalism as "centripetal federalism", ensuring stability through a strong
Centre while granting States participatory space.
Key Features of Federalism
1. Division of Powers - Powers divided through the Union List, State List, and Concurrent List (Seventh
Schedule).
2. Dual Polity - Division of governance between the Union and States, each with its own government.
3. Written Constitution - Provides clarity and certainty in the division of powers.
4. Supremacy of the Constitution - Both Centre and States are bound by the Constitution as the supreme
law.
5. Rigid Constitution - Certain provisions can only be amended by special procedure, requiring consent of
States.
6. Independent Judiciary - The Supreme Court acts as guardian and interpreter of the Constitution to
resolve Centre-State disputes.
7. Bicameralism at Centre - Rajya Sabha represents States in the Union legislature.
8. Financial Autonomy - Distribution of financial resources and functions through Finance Commission
and constitutional provisions.

9. Authority of States in Normal Times - States have autonomy in matters under the State List, subject to
constitutional limits.
10. Unity with Diversity - Indian federalism accommodates linguistic, cultural, and regional diversities
while maintaining national unity. Eg. Article 371
Unitary Features of the Indian Constitution
1. States are Not Indestructible (Article 3) - The Parliament has the authority to create, merge, or alter
state boundaries and names without requiring state consent.
2. Emergency Provisions -
a. National Emergency (Article 352) - The President can assume extraordinary powers in case of war,
external aggression, or armed rebellion.
b. President's Rule (Article 356) - If a state government fails to function per constitutional provisions,
the President can assume direct control, making the federal structure temporarily unitary.
3. Strong Union Legislature -
a. Residuary Powers (Article 248) - Parliament holds exclusive authority over subjects not listed in the
Union, State, or Concurrent lists.
b. State List Override (Article 249) - The Rajya Sabha can authorize Parliament to legislate on a State
List subject in the national interest.
4. Law Supremacy (Article 254) - In case of a conflict between state and central laws on a Concurrent List
subject, the central law prevails.
5. Strong Union Executive (Article 256) - State governments must comply with laws made by Parliament,
and the Union can direct state executives to enforce them.
6. Single Citizenship - Unlike other federations, India provides for only one citizenship, ensuring uniform
rights and duties for all citizens across states.
7. All-India Services - The IAS, IPS, and IFS are centrally controlled services that operate in states,
strengthening the Union's role in state administration.
8. Integrated Judiciary - India follows a single judicial system with the Supreme Court at the apex,
ensuring uniform interpretation of laws across the country.
Significance of Federalism - PIES Framework
Political Significance
1. Unity in Diversity - Eg. Reorganisation of states on linguistic lines (1956).
2. Checks and Balances - Prevents excessive centralisation. Eg. 7th schedule
3. Adaptability to Regional Aspirations: Eg. The formation of Telangana in 2014 to accommodate regional
demands for administrative efficiency and cultural identity.
Institutional Significance
1. Conflict Resolution - Constitutional mechanisms like Inter-State Council and Supreme Court
adjudication.
2. Rajya Sabha Role - Provides States a voice in national policymaking.
3. Cooperative Federalism - Institutions like the GST Council ensure joint decision-making.
Economic Significance
1. Balanced Development - Allows States to pursue region-specific growth strategies. Eg. Gujarat
investor's summit
2. Fiscal Federalism - Finance Commission ensures equitable resource devolution (Eg. 16th FC kept states'
share to 41%).
3. Policy Innovation - States act as laboratories of democracy; Eg. Midday Meal Scheme in Tamil Nadu
later scaled nationally.

Social Significance
1. Inclusive Governance - Enhances representation of diverse communities at local, state, and national
levels.
2. Social Justice - Enables States to frame affirmative action policies based on local needs. Eg. Tamil
Nadu's 69% reservation model.
3. Cultural and Linguistic Preservation: Eg. Tamil Nadu enforcing 2 language formula
4. National Integration - Balances regional identity with national citizenship, strengthening unity.
Asymmetric Federalism
Asymmetric federalism refers to an unequal distribution of powers and relationships in the political,
administrative, and fiscal spheres among federal units within a federation.
According to Riker (1975), federalism is an outcome of rational bargain among various constituents.
Key Aspects:
1. Dual Asymmetry - It exists in both vertical (Centre vs. States) and horizontal (among States/UTs)
dimensions.
2. Self-Rule & Shared Rule - India follows a "salad bowl" approach, recognizing distinct cultural
identities and permitting self-governance within a unified structure.
3. Founding Vision allows for regional autonomy while ensuring a strong central authority when needed.
Vertical Asymmetry (Between Centre and States)
1. Article 3 -Parliament can alter state boundaries and names, without state consent
2. Single Citizenship - Unlike US, India has only one citizenship for uniform national identity
3. Articles 352 & 356 empower the Centre to take direct control of states during Emergency.
4. Article 248 - Residuary legislative powers rest solely with the Parliament.
5. Governor Appointment - appointed by the President & serves as the Centre's representative
6. 7th Schedule - On subjects in the Concurrent List, Parliament laws override state laws.
7. All India Services: ultimate control lies with the centre.
Horizontal Asymmetry (Among States/UTs)
1. Articles 371A-371J - Provide special provisions and autonomy to certain states.
2. Schedule 5 & 6 - self-governance in tribal regions.
3. Article 370 (Revoked in 2019) - special status to J&K with substantial autonomy
4. Unequal representation in the Rajya Sabha: The allocation of seats based on population.
Fiscal Asymmetry
1. Centre controls buoyant tax sources (income tax, corporate tax, GST share). States bear higher
expenditure responsibilities (health, education, welfare, agriculture).
2. Horizontal Devolution - criteria of income disparity, demographic trends, and ecological performance.
3. Article 293: States' borrowing is subject to Centre's consent.
4. CSS - Central share of 90% for Special Category States (60% for others)
5. Special Category Status - Granted by the National Development Council (NDC) to states with hilly
terrain, low population density, or economic backwardness.

Need and Significance
1. Political Significance
a. Rekha Saxena describes Indian Asymmetric Federalism as 'Self-rule within shared rule'
b. National Integration - tailored autonomy for preventing alienation and separatism. Eg. Article 371F
for Sikkim's accession (1975).
c. Conflict Resolution - Eg. Mizoram Accord (1986) under Sixth Schedule.
2. Institutional Significance
a. Decentralised Governance Eg. Autonomous District Councils under Sixth Schedule
b. Democratic Representation - Eg. Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC, 2020) for STs.
3. Economic Significance
a. Bridging Regional Imbalances - Eg. N-E states get 90:10 funding ratio under CSS.
b. Equity in resource distribution - Hill states like Himachal, UK get higher devolution.
4. Social Significance
a. Cultural Autonomy - Eg. Nagaland's customary laws protected under Article 371A
b. Inclusivity & Harmony - Prevents majority domination and fosters trust.
c. Unity in Diversity - Builds confidence in communities within a national framework. Eg. Goa
retained Portuguese Civil Code post-integration.
Issues
1. Political
a. Asymmetric provisions have weakened due to legislative manoeuvres and judicial
reinterpretations, causing a centralising drift. Eg. Abrogation of Article 370
b. Politicisation of Asymmetry -Eg. Demand for special category status by Bihar
c. Threat to National Unity - Excessive asymmetry encourages identity politics and secessionist
tendencies. Eg. Demands for greater autonomy in Manipur hills
d. Representation Imbalances - Delimitation reducing clout of southern states
2. Institutional
a. Complexity in Governance - Eg. Erstwhile J&K had separate civil and criminal procedure, no
reservations for SC/STs
b. Administrative Complexity causes bureaucratic delays and weak coordination. Eg. Delays in fund
utilisation in tribal councils
c. Ambiguities in Provisions - Lack of clarity in constitutional provisions leads to legal disputes. Eg.
Article 370 case in Supreme Court
3. Economic
a. Uneven Development - Fiscal and institutional asymmetries deepen regional inequalities. Eg. NE
states dependent on central grants
4. Social
a. Ethnic Tensions - Autonomous regions face internal conflicts between different ethnic groups.
b. Social Integration Challenges - Special statuses may foster regionalism instead of integration. Eg.
Gorkhaland demand in West Bengal
Way Forward
1. As recommended by the Sarkaria Commission (1983) and NCRWC (2000), the ISC should be made an
independent body under Article 263 to resolve Centre-state disputes.
2. Establishing clear guidelines for Special Category Status (SCS).

collectively to address common challenges through collaboration and shared responsibilities.

cooperative, requiring mutual interaction between Centre and States.

Competitive Federalism
Competitive federalism refers to the healthy competition among different levels of government within a
federal system to enhance efficiency, governance, and resource attraction.
Steps Taken Towards Competitive Federalism
1. Monthly rankings of 112 Aspirational Districts create "fame and shame" pressure on district collectors
to improve social indicators.
2. Ease of Doing Business (EoDB) Rankings - DPIIT ranks states based on their implementation of the
Business Reform Action Plan (BRAP).
3. Fiscal incentives - 16th FC has linked a portion of central grants to state performance in areas like fiscal
discipline, forest conservation, and implementation of power sector reforms.
4. Sectoral Performance Indices
a. Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) India Index: Ranks states on 16 goals, pushing them to
achieve universal targets like poverty reduction and clean energy.
b. India Innovation Index: Measures the innovation ecosystem across states, encouraging a
"knowledge economy."
c. Export Preparedness Index (EPI): Identifies the export potential and performance of states, pushing
landlocked and coastal states to build better logistics.
d. State Health Index: Compares health outcomes, driving states like Uttar Pradesh and Bihar to bridge
gaps with leaders like Kerala.
e. Composite Water Management Index - Measuring progress on water resource management.
5. Reforms in Labor Laws and Land Acquisition - States have reformed labor laws and land acquisition
processes to attract investment
6. PM Gati Shakti: States compete to align their industrial corridors and logistic parks with the national
master plan to receive central funding and attract multinational manufacturing hubs
7. Competitive Social Welfare Models - Eg. "Delhi Education Model" & "Odisha 5T Transformation" has
forced other states to rethink school infrastructure and digital governance to stay politically and
economically competitive.
Significance of Competitive Federalism
1. Investment Attraction: Eg. labor reforms and land subsidies to attract subsidies
2. Efficiency in Service Delivery: Public rankings create reputational pressure on governments to improve
basic services. Eg. Jal Jeevan Mission dashboard.
3. Innovation in Public Policy: States act as "policy laboratories," testing unique welfare models that are
later scaled nationally. Eg. Odisha's 5T school transformation model (2025)

Challenges in Competitive Federalism
1. Regional Inequality: Resource-rich southern and western states attract more FDI than "BIMARU" states.
Eg. five states account for nearly 70% of India's total FDI equity inflows.
2. North-South Divide over the distribution of central taxes and political representation. Eg. southern
state's protest against population-based criteria under 16th FC.
3. Race to the Bottom: States may aggressively cut corporate taxes or dilute labor protections to underbid
their neighbors.
4. Inter-State Trust Deficit: High-intensity competition for projects (like semiconductor fabs) can sour
cooperative relations between neighboring states.
5. One-Size-Fits-All Indices: Rankings often use uniform parameters that ignore the unique geographical
or historical constraints of hill/tribal states.
6. Data Manipulation Risks: High pressure to rank well can lead states to report "window-dressed" data to
central agencies.
7. Limited Revenue Autonomy: Post-GST, states have limited tax-raising powers, making it hard to fund
the infrastructure needed to compete.
8. Political Bias Allegations: Opposition-ruled states often allege that central rankings and fund
allocations are politically motivated or biased.
