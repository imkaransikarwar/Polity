Prime Minister
Lord Morley described the Prime Minister as "primus inter pares" (first among equals) and "key stone of the
cabinet arch".
Ramsay Muir described the Prime Minister as "the steering wheel of the ship of the state."
Powers and Functions:
With Respect to the President
1. Link between the President and the Council of Ministers (Article 78).
2. Advice on Appointment:
a. Advises the President on the appointment of other ministers.
b. Recommends the allocation and reshuffling of portfolios among ministers.
3. Advisory Role:
a. Advises the President on summoning and proroguing of Parliament and dissolution of the Lok Sabha.
b. Advises in appointments of constitutional authorities (Eg. Attorney General, CAG, Election
Commissioners, etc.).
With Respect to the Council of Ministers
1. Head of the Council: The Prime Minister is the head of the Council of Ministers and decides its agenda.
2. Leader and Coordinator:
a. Guides, directs, and coordinates the activities of all ministers.
b. Can ask any minister to resign or recommend dismissal to the President.
3. Chairman of Meetings: Presides over meetings of the Cabinet and influences decisions.

PMO
Origin
1. 1947–1977: Established as the Prime Minister's Secretariat (PMS) to provide secretarial assistance to
Jawaharlal Nehru.
2. 1977: Renamed the Prime Minister's Office (PMO) during the tenure of Morarji Desai to project a less
"secretive" and more "office-like" image.
3. Evolution: Under Lal Bahadur Shastri, the role of the Principal Secretary was created, shifting the PMO
from a mere clerical body to a high-level policy advisory unit.
Key Functions
1. Secretarial Assistance: Provides the PM with technical and administrative support for managing the
Union Cabinet and Parliament.
2. Inter-Ministerial Coordination: resolving inter-departmental disputes and ensuring policy synergy.
3. Policy Monitoring: Tracks the progress of "big-ticket" infrastructure projects (via the Project
Monitoring Group) and flagship schemes like Gati Shakti.
4. National Security & Foreign Policy: Houses the National Security Advisor (NSA) and coordinates with
intelligence agencies and the Ministry of External Affairs.
5. Public Grievances: Manages the CPGRAMS portal and the Prime Minister's National Relief Fund
(PMNRF).

Major Criticisms
1. Over-Centralization of Power: Critics argue that the PMO has become a "Super-Cabinet"
2. The PMO has largely eclipsed the Cabinet Secretariat, which is the traditional coordinating body under
the Rules of Business.
3. Parallel Bureaucracy: The office is seen as creating a "shadow government" where unelected advisors
override the expertise of senior civil servants.
4. Unitary Tilt in Federalism: Eg. Frequent direct meetings between the PMO and District Collectors have
bypassed state Chief Secretaries.
5. Opaqueness in Decision-Making: the internal deliberation process within the PMO remains shielded
from RTI and public scrutiny.
6. Politicization of Bureaucracy: High-profile retired bureaucrats are frequently inducted into the PMO,
leading to allegations of a "committed bureaucracy."
Way Forward
1. Restoring Cabinet Supremacy: Re-empower the Cabinet Secretariat to handle routine coordination,
allowing the PMO to focus strictly on the PM's personal secretarial needs.
2. Formalizing Consultation: Institutionalize a "Consultation Charter" where the PMO must seek
mandatory input from State Governments before intervening in state-list subjects.
3. Parliamentary Oversight: Create a dedicated Standing Committee or a sub-committee under the Public
Accounts Committee to specifically audit the functioning of the PMO.
Should India Adopt a Presidential Form of Government?
Arguments in Favour of Presidential System
1. Separation of Powers - presidentialism ensures an independent legislature, preventing executive
dominance
2. Directly Elected Executive - ensuring legitimacy from people's mandate.
3. Stability of Tenure - Fixed tenure not vulnerable to coalition collapses or legislative whims.
4. Cabinet of Talents - Freedom to appoint ministers from outside legislatures, bringing in experts and
technocrats.
5. Clear Accountability - Voters can judge an individual leader on performance in governance rather than
political manoeuvring.
6. Decisive Leadership - Direct election gives a strong mandate for effective governance.
Shashi Tharoor's Criticism of the Parliamentary System in India
- Unqualified Legislators - The system has created a breed of legislators "largely unqualified to
legislate," who contest elections only to gain executive power.
- Government Instability - Governments are dependent on fickle legislative majorities, forcing them to
prioritise politics over policy or performance.
- Weak Political Parties - Parties have become "shifting alliances of selfish interests," lacking coherent
ideologies.
- Coalition Compulsions - Coalition governments are obliged to cater to the lowest common
denominator, diluting governance effectiveness.
- Focus on Staying in Power - Instead of governance, governments spend energy on managing
alliances and survival.
Arguments Against Presidential System
1. Ambedkar in Constituent Assembly - Warned presidential system may "degenerate into dictatorship."

Presidential Reference
Recently, The President invoked Article 143(1) of the Constitution to seek the Supreme Court's opinion on the
scope of powers of Governors and the President under Articles 200 and 201.
The reference follows a recent Supreme Court ruling that set clear timelines for Governors and the President
to act on bills passed by state legislatures. The verdict also introduced the idea of 'deemed consent' to address
delays caused by gubernatorial inaction.
It is a power that the President has invoked on at least 15 occasions since 1950.
In 1951, India's first President Rajendra Prasad made the first reference to the Supreme Court in relation to
provisions of Delhi Laws Act, 1912, the Ajmer-Merwara (Extension of Laws) Act, 1947 and Part C of the States
(Laws) Act of 1950.
Article 143 (Advisory jurisdiction of SC)
Article 143(1) - Discretionary Reference
1. The President may refer to the Supreme Court a question of law or fact that is of public importance.
2. The Supreme Court may choose to answer the reference or not.
3. The Court's opinion is advisory, not binding, but carries significant constitutional weight.
Article 143(2) - Mandatory Reference
1. If any question arises relating to pre-constitutional treaties or agreements, the President must refer it
to the Court.
2. The Court is obligated to give its opinion in this case.
Article 145(3)
requires any such reference to be heard by five judges, after which the SC returns the reference to the
President with the majority opinion.
Important Judgments
- In re Kerala Education Bill (1958) - First use of Article 143(1). Set precedent for judicial restraint and
clarified that advisory opinion is not binding.
- In re Berubari Union (1960)

Significance of Article 143
1. Under the Constitution, the President acts on the aid and advice of the Cabinet. The advisory jurisdiction
allows her the means to seek independent advice on constitutional matters.
2. Bridge Between Executive and Judiciary - consultation with the Supreme Court strengthens
coordination and clarity between constitutional organs.
3. Clarifies Constitutional Ambiguities - Helps in interpreting grey areas of the Constitution, laws, or
treaties.
4. Prevents Legal Disputes - Advisory opinions can pre-empt future litigation by clarifying
constitutional questions before conflicts arise.
5. Democratic and Transparent Governance - Encourages legal scrutiny of proposed policies or actions
without adversarial litigation.
6. Non-Binding Yet Influential - Though not binding, SC's opinion carries great moral and constitutional
authority, guiding executive and legislative decisions.
7. Used in Landmark Cases - Played a key role in Berubari case (1960), Special Courts Bill (1979), and
Cauvery dispute (1992) - shaping constitutional jurisprudence.
Major Issues
1. Non-Binding Nature of Opinion
2. Discretion to Decline Reference - Eg. In the Ram Janmabhoomi reference (1993), the Court declined to
answer, citing lack of a legal issue.
3. Possibility of Political Misuse - Executive may use Article 143 to politically deflect controversial issues or
influence ongoing public debates.
4. Risks converting the Court into a consultative body for the executive.
5. Lack of Procedural Guidelines - The Constitution does not specify detailed procedures or timelines for
the Court to respond to a reference.
6. Overlaps with Judicial Review - Advisory opinions under Article 143 may influence or pre-judge issues
that could later come before the Court in a judicial capacity.
Way Forward
1. Make Advisory Opinion Binding in Limited Cases, such as, Constitutional interpretation, Federal
disputes, Emergency powers.
2. Introduce Time-Bound Procedure for the Supreme Court
3. Establish clear guidelines (either through statute or SC rules) on: What constitutes a valid "question of
public importance" and When the Court may decline a reference.
4. Publish Detailed Reasons for Refusal
5. Require the government to table an action taken report in Parliament on Court's opinion.

SC judgment on Article 200 and 201
A Division Bench of Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan ruled that Tamil Nadu Governor R.N. Ravi's
withholding of 10 state bills was "illegal" and "erroneous."
The Court clarified that the Governor's powers under Article 200 are limited and must be exercised within
constitutional bounds.
Invoking Article 142, the Bench held that the pending and reserved bills are to be treated as having received
"deemed assent."
Article 200 (Governor's Powers)
1. Three Exclusive Options: A Governor may (i) grant assent, (ii) withhold assent and return for
reconsideration, or (iii) reserve the Bill for the President. No "pocket" or absolute veto exists.
2. Time‑limits Imposed:
a. Withholding or Reserving (on Council advice): within 1 month.
b. Withholding against advice: return within 3 months, with reasons.
c. Re‑passed Bills: assent immediately, within 1 month.
3. Judicial Review: Governor's inaction or misuse (pocket veto) is justiciable; courts can examine
arbitrariness or malafide.
Article 201 (President's Powers)
1. Three‑Month Deadline to decide on Bills reserved by Governors. Delays beyond must be accompanied
by written reasons.
2. No Pocket Veto: President cannot indefinitely withhold assent; "deemed assent" if unreasonably delayed.
3. Proviso to Article 201 (return‑and‑repass) must not be used piecemeal to delay legislation. Once
re‑passed, the President must grant or withhold assent with clear reasons.
4. Judicial Review: limited scrutiny where Union interest predominates; deeper review where State
primacy and Cabinet advice conflict.
Significance of the Judgment
1. Curtails Arbitrary Use of Article 200
2. Prevents politicization of office of Governor and President
3. Protects separation of powers - Reclaims the primacy of the legislature in lawmaking by limiting
executive interference.
4. Establishes Time Limits for Decision on Bills - ends the practice of "pocket veto"
5. Strengthens Legislative Supremacy in States
6. Concept of 'Deemed Assent' Introduced - Sets a powerful precedent for ensuring legislative continuity
and constitutional efficiency.
7. Promotes Constitutional Morality - Reinforces the expectation that constitutional authorities must act
in good faith, and not stall democratic processes.
Major Issues in the Judgment
1. Judicial Overreach -Eg. legal doctrine of deemed assent not explicitly found in Constitution
2. Ambiguity - Unclear how "deemed assent" will be operationalized or enforced
3. The judgment reaffirms limited discretion for Governors, but doesn't fully define the scope of
"constitutional discretion" under Article 200.
4. One-Size-Fits-All Federal Application - does not account for varying political, administrative, or legal
complexities across different regions.

Constitutional Provisions:
1. Article 74: Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President. This advice is binding on the President
after the 44th Amendment to the Constitution.
2. Article 75: Composition of the Council of Ministers; their appointment, tenure, responsibility, etc.
3. Article 77: Conduct of business of the Government of India.
4. Article 88: Ministers can speak and participate in either House of Parliament, joint sittings, and
committees, but cannot vote.
5. 91st Amendment Act, 2003: Limited the size of the Council of Ministers to 15% of Lok Sabha or state
assembly members, to prevent excessive appointments.
Powers and Functions:
1. Collective Responsibility: Under Article 75(3), the Council is collectively accountable to the Lok Sabha
for all executive decisions.
2. Formulation of National Policy: The Council deliberates on and decides all major domestic and foreign
policies for the nation.
3. Aiding and Advising the President: Under Article 74, the Council provides binding advice to the
President on all executive functions.
4. Legislative Leadership: Ministers draft and introduce almost all major government bills and steer them
through both Houses.
5. Financial Management and Budgeting: The Council is responsible for preparing the Union Budget and
ensuring fiscal discipline across ministries.
6. Supreme Executive Control: Each minister oversees the administration of their assigned department,
ensuring the implementation of government programs.
7. Coordination of Administration: The Council ensures synergy between different departments to avoid
policy conflicts and overlapping jurisdictions.

Cabinet
The Cabinet is the core of the Council of Ministers and the highest decision-making authority in the Indian
politico- administrative system. It was not included in the original text of the Constitution and the 44th
Constitutional Amendment Act,1978 inserted 'Cabinet 'into Article 352 of the Constitution.
Significance of the Cabinet System in India
1. Real Centre of Executive Power - It drives national policy, handles administrative affairs, and directs all
ministries and departments.
2. Principle of Division of Labour and effective delegation - Enhances specialization, expertise, and
sectoral focus within governance.
3. Supreme Policy Formulation: It serves as the highest authority for determining national priorities and
long-term strategic goals.
4. Inter-Ministerial Coordination: It resolves jurisdictional overlaps between different ministries to ensure
a unified "Whole of Government" approach. Eg. PM Gati Shakti
5. All major government bills and constitutional amendments must receive Cabinet approval before being
introduced in Parliament.
6. Financial Oversight: The Cabinet approves the Union Budget and all major public expenditure proposals
exceeding specific fiscal thresholds.
7. Binding Advice to President: Under Article 74, its advice is binding, making it the de facto wielder of the
President's powers.
8. It determines India's stance on international treaties, global alliances, and strategic defense agreements.

Issues with Cabinet System:
1. Top-Down approach in decision making: overshadows other ministers, limiting diverse inputs in
policy-making.
2. Lack of Transparency: Cabinet meetings are confidential, reducing transparency about
decision-making processes.
3. Concentration of Power in the Prime Minister - Reduces the Cabinet to a rubber stamp in strong
majority governments.
4. Erosion of Collective Responsibility - Cabinet decisions are often shaped by a small inner circle or
PMO, not through broad ministerial deliberation.
5. The Prime Minister's Office (PMO) functions as a parallel power center, weakening Cabinet committees
and inter-ministerial consultation.
6. Coalition Compulsions and Policy Paralysis due to political bargaining, compromises, and external
pressures.
7. Limited Parliamentary Oversight - Eg. Money Bills and Ordinances are often passed without serious
discussion, sidestepping parliamentary scrutiny.
8. Administrative Overload: Eg. In 2026, over 40% of Cabinet agenda items were reported to be
"procedural" rather than policy-oriented.
9. The "Kitchen Cabinet" Phenomenon: Crucial decisions are often made by a small "inner circle" of 2-3
ministers, sidelining the larger Cabinet.
Way Forward to Strengthen the System
1. Formalizing Consultation: Adopting the Punchhi Commission's recommendation for mandatory
state-level feedback via the Inter-State Council.
2. Empowering Ministry-level Research: Each Cabinet Minister should have a "Policy Unit" of domain
experts to reduce dependence on the PMO.
3. Strict Parliamentary Sittings: Increase the number of mandatory Parliament days to 100+ annually to
ensure Cabinet accountability.
4. Shadow Cabinet Tradition: Encourage the opposition to form a "Shadow Cabinet" to provide
constructive alternatives and rigorous scrutiny. Eg. in UK/Australia
5. Reforming the Rules of Business: Update the 1961 Rules to clearly define the boundaries between PMO
coordination and Cabinet authority.
Constitution (One Hundred and Thirtieth Amendment) Bill, 2025
The primary aim of the Bill is to uphold "constitutional morality" by preventing the PM, CM, and Ministers
from continuing in office while in judicial or police custody for an extended period.
Key Provisions
1. If a Minister is arrested and remains in custody for 30 consecutive days on serious criminal charges
(offenses punishable by 5 years or more), they will automatically lose their office.
2. Reinstatement Clause: If the leader is later granted bail or cleared through the legal process, they may
resume their position.

3. Universal Application: Unlike previous historical attempts (like the 39th Amendment) which sought to
shield high offices, this Bill specifically includes the PM within its purview.
4. Two related bills, the Union Territories (Amendment) Bill, 2025, and the Jammu and Kashmir
Reorganisation (Amendment) Bill, 2025, were introduced simultaneously to apply these rules to UTs
like Delhi, Puducherry, and J&K.
Arguments In Favour
1. Constitutional Morality - Prevents leaders from continuing in power while facing serious criminal
charges
2. Good Governance - Removes "governance from jail" anomaly & ensures executive accountability.
3. Public Trust - Strong stance against criminalisation of politics enhances institutional trust.
4. Bridges Legal Gap - RPA, 1951 disqualifies only post-conviction; this Bill addresses interim
pre-conviction situations.
5. Aligns with International Best Practices - UK Ministerial Code, South African Constitution (Section
96) - public officials facing serious charges step aside.
6. Addresses Lily Thomas (2013), Manoj Narula (2014) Spirit - SC has long called for stricter standards for
Ministers.
7. Anti-Corruption Push - Highlights zero tolerance against corruption in high offices.
8. As per HM Amit Shah, it is an "insult to democracy" for secretaries and officials to take orders from a
jailed leader.
Arguments Against
1. Arrest vs. Conviction: violates the principle of "innocent until proven guilty" by imposing a severe
penalty based on mere arrest/detention rather than a court conviction.
2. Legal experts suggest the Bill might be challenged for violating the "Rule of Law" and the "Equality
Code" (Article 14).
3. Weakens PM's Constitutional Prerogative: In Manoj Narula v. UoI (2014), SC held selection of Ministers
is "constitutional prerogative" of PM/CM. The Bill limits this discretion.
4. Unequal Standards - Creates asymmetry: MPs/MLAs removed only on conviction, but Ministers on
detention.
5. Parliamentary Confidence Eroded: Displaces legislative majority principle under Articles 75 & 164 with
police/judicial discretion.
6. Governance Destabilised: Cycles of resignation and reinstatement weaken stability and fracture
coalitions.
7. Opportunism Encouraged: Enables ruling parties to weaponise detentions to eliminate rivals.
8. Authoritarian Tilt: Empowers law enforcement over Parliament, reflecting abusive constitutionalism.
9. "Revolving Door" Problem: The provision allowing reappointment after release from custody could lead
to cycles of resignation and reinstatement
Way Forward
1. Address Presumption of Innocence - Consider triggering removal at charge-framing stage (Law
Commission 244th Report) instead of mere arrest.
2. Strengthen independence of ED, CBI through institutional reforms to remove fear of political
weaponisation.
3. Time-Bound Trials - Fast-track courts for politicians to conclude trials within 1 year to avoid prolonged
uncertainty.
4. Mandatory judicial review of detention before automatic removal.
