| How the executive makes law | **1.** The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers issue rules, orders and executive instructions only to carry out laws already passed by Parliament. They cannot create a new law.<br>**2.** The single power to legislate directly is the Ordinance (Art 123; Art 213 for a Governor), issued by the President on the advice of the Cabinet when Parliament is not in session.<br>**3.** An Ordinance has the same force as an Act, but is a strictly temporary measure. | **1.** A large part of American policy is made by Executive Order, signed by the President and in force the moment it is signed, with no vote in Congress.<br>**2.** The Order stands until Congress legislates it away or a court strikes it down. | In this respect the President of the USA does what the Parliament of India does. India's nearest equivalent, the Ordinance, is a stop-gap, not a substitute for legislation. |
| Checks against an arbitrary executive act | **1.** An Ordinance must be laid before both Houses and lapses six weeks after reassembly unless both Houses approve it (Art 123(2)).<br>**2.** Parliament can simply let it die; no special majority is needed to kill it.<br>**3.** It is open to judicial review, and re-promulgation without placing it before the House is a fraud on the Constitution (D.C. Wadhwa, 1987; reaffirmed in Krishna Kumar Singh, 2017). | **1.** Congress may pass a law revoking the Order, but the President will veto that law, so Congress must find a two-thirds majority in both the House and the Senate - politically very difficult.<br>**2.** Federal courts may strike an Order down if it violates the Constitution or goes beyond the authority Congress granted.<br>**3.** In Learning Resources v. Trump (20 Feb 2026) the Supreme Court, by 6-3, held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not authorise the tariffs; the trade court then ordered refunds of over $100 billion to importers.<br>**4.** The President obeyed the ruling, publicly attacked it, and re-imposed similar tariffs under other trade statutes. Hundreds of challenges to other Orders remain pending. | The burden runs the opposite way. An Ordinance dies unless Parliament approves it; an Executive Order lives unless Congress finds two thirds or a court strikes it down. |
| Structure of the legislature | **1.** Parliament: Lok Sabha, maximum 550, term five years, dissolvable; Rajya Sabha, maximum 250, permanent, one third retiring every second year on six year terms. | **1.** Congress: House of Representatives, 435 members, two year terms; Senate, 100 members, six year terms, one third elected every second year. | The Rajya Sabha is not subject to dissolution, and neither is the US Senate. Only the popularly elected lower house is. |
| Representation in the upper house | **1.** Unequal, in rough proportion to population, under the Fourth Schedule. Uttar Pradesh has 31 seats, Sikkim has one.<br>**2.** Members are elected by the elected members of the State Legislative Assemblies. | **1.** Equal. Two Senators for every State, whatever its size, elected directly by the people of the State since the 17th Amendment, 1913. | Equal representation of units is a classic federal feature. India's departure from it is a unitary trait, and is why India is called quasi federal. |
