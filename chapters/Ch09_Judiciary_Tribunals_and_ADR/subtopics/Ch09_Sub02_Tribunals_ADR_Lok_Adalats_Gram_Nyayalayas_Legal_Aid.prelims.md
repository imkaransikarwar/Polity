## CHAPTER 17
TRIBUNALS, ADR AND LEGAL AID
THEME ANALYSIS PYQ pattern for this chapter
1. REPEATS

#### A. Constitutional Basis of Tribunals
- Articles 323A and 323B were inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 [2009]
- Article 323A: Parliament alone, and only for recruitment and service matters of public servants; Article 323B:
Parliament or a State legislature, for a listed set of subjects
- Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985; the Central Administrative Tribunal began on 1 November 1985 under Rajiv
Gandhi, not Lal Bahadur Shastri [2009]
- Members are drawn from both the judicial and the administrative streams [2009]
- L. Chandra Kumar (1997): review under Articles 226, 227 and 32 is Basic Structure, so tribunal orders stay
reviewable by a Division Bench of the High Court

#### B. Lok Adalats
- Statutory footing in Chapter VI of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 [2009, 2010]
- Takes both pending and pre litigation matters, and civil as well as criminal ones, the only bar being non
compoundable offences [2010]
- The award is deemed a decree of a civil court and no appeal lies against it [2009]
- Matrimonial and family disputes are regularly taken up, which is the standard distractor [2009]
- A bench is not composed of judicial officers alone; Permanent Lok Adalats cover public utility services up to Rs 1
crore and may decide on merits [2010]

#### C. Gram Nyayalayas
- Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008, in force from 2 October 2009, one for every intermediate level Panchayat
- Presided over by a Nyayadhikari with the status, salary and powers of a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class,
appointed by the State in consultation with the High Court
- A mobile court that sits in villages, with both civil and criminal jurisdiction, so the claim that it hears only civil cases is
wrong [2016]
- Not bound by the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, and local social activists may serve as conciliators [2016]

#### D. Legal Aid and the Legal Services Authorities
- Article 39A, a Directive Principle inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976, is the source of free legal aid [2013,
2020]
- NALSA under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, with the Chief Justice of India as Patron in Chief, issues
guidelines to the State authorities [2013]
- The tiers: State Legal Services Authority under the Chief Justice of the High Court, District authority under the
District Judge, and a Taluk committee below it
- Section 12 entitlement list: SCs and STs, victims of trafficking or begar, women and children, persons with mental
illness or disability, industrial workmen and persons in custody [2020]
- The income ceiling and the transgender and senior citizen options are the recurring trap [2020]
- Also tested: Zero FIR under the BNSS [2026]
2. HOW THE FORMAT HAS CHANGED
- 2009 to 2016: two statement sets on CAT, Lok Adalats, NALSA and Gram Nyayalayas [2009, 2010, 2013, 2016]
- 2020: an eligibility code set on free legal aid [2020]
- 2026: three statements on Zero FIR under the BNSS [2026]
3. WHAT IS BEING ASKED NOW
- The new criminal law codes: Zero FIR [2026]
- Who qualifies for free legal aid [2020]

Tribunals[PRELIMS 2009]
1. Articles 323A and 323B were inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.
2. Article 323A enables Parliament alone to provide for administrative tribunals for disputes relating to recruitment and
conditions of service of public servants of the Union and the States;
3. Article 323B lets Parliament or a State legislature set up tribunals for other matters: taxation, foreign exchange,
industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, elections, essential goods, urban property ceilings.
Under Article 323A, the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 was enacted, and the CAT began work on 1 November
1985, under PM Rajiv Gandhi (not Lal Bahadur Shastri).
4. Its members are drawn from both the judicial and the administrative streams.
5. In L. Chandra Kumar (1997) the Supreme Court held that judicial review by the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and
by the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the Basic Structure, so tribunal decisions are subject to review by a
Division Bench of the concerned High Court.
6. Orders of the Central Administrative Tribunal and other tribunals are amenable to the writ jurisdiction of the High Court
within whose territorial jurisdiction the tribunal is situated (L. Chandra Kumar, 1997);
7. a High Court's power of superintendence under Article 227 extends over all tribunals in its territory, except military
tribunals.
Lok Adalats[PRELIMS 2010,2009]
1. Given statutory status by Chapter VI (Sections 19-22) of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987.
2. A Lok Adalat may take up both (i) a case pending before a court and (ii) a matter at the pre-litigation stage falling within
the jurisdiction of that court - so it is not confined to either.
3. It may settle civil as well as criminal matters, the only bar being offences that are non-compoundable under any law;
4. matrimonial and family disputes are regularly taken up.
5. Its award is deemed to be a decree of a civil court, is final and binding on all parties, and no appeal lies against it before
any court.
6. A bench is not composed only of judicial officers - it typically has a serving or retired judicial officer together with a
lawyer and a social worker.
7. National Lok Adalats are held on a single day across all courts in the country.
8. Permanent Lok Adalats under Section 22B deal with disputes relating to public utility services (transport, postal,
telegraph, power, water supply, insurance, hospitals, sanitation, etc.), with pecuniary jurisdiction up to Rs 1 crore;
9. uniquely, if conciliation fails they may decide the dispute on merits, but they cannot try an offence.
Gram Nyayalayas[PRELIMS 2016]
1. The Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 (in force from 2 October 2009) provides for a Gram Nyayalaya for every Panchayat at
the intermediate level, or a group of contiguous intermediate-level Panchayats, with its headquarters at the intermediate
Panchayat headquarters.
2. It is presided over by a Nyayadhikari appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court, who has
the same status, salary and powers as a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class.
3. It is conceived as a mobile court: the Nyayadhikari periodically visits villages and disposes of cases at places other than
its headquarters.

4. It exercises both civil and criminal jurisdiction over matters listed in the First and Second Schedules (criminal cases
being tried by summary procedure), so it is wrong to say it hears only civil cases.
5. It is not bound by the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 and is guided by the principles of natural justice, and it must endeavour
to settle disputes by conciliation through conciliators appointed for that purpose, which permits the use of local social
activists as mediators/conciliators.
Legal Aid & Legal Services Authorities[PRELIMS 2020,2013]
1. Article 39A (DPSP, inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976) obliges the State to secure that the operation of the
legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity and to provide free legal aid so that no citizen is denied
justice by reason of economic or other disability.
2. The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (brought into force 9 November 1995) constituted NALSA, with the Chief
Justice of India as Patron-in-Chief and a serving Supreme Court judge nominated by the President as Executive
Chairman;
3. the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee handles matters before the Supreme Court.
4. NALSA lays down policies and issues guidelines and directions to the State Legal Services Authorities for
implementing legal aid programmes and schemes throughout the country and disburses funds to them.
5. Section 12 entitles the following to free and competent legal services: members of SCs and STs;
6. victims of trafficking in human beings or begar (Article 23);
7. women and children;
8. persons with mental illness or disability;
9. victims of mass disaster, ethnic or caste violence, flood, drought, earthquake or industrial disaster;
10. industrial workmen;
11. persons in custody (including in a protective home, juvenile home or psychiatric hospital); and persons whose annual
income is below the ceiling prescribed by the State Government (Rs 5,00,000 for cases before the Supreme Court;
commonly Rs 3,00,000 in most States, lower in some).
12. Entitlement is thus not automatic for all senior citizens or for all members of a backward class - an income test applies.
13. The State Legal Services Authority is headed by the Chief Justice of the High Court as Patron-in-Chief, with a serving
or retired High Court judge as Executive Chairman nominated by the Governor in consultation with the Chief Justice.
14. Every High Court also has a High Court Legal Services Committee for cases before it.
15. District Legal Services Authority in every district, chaired by the District Judge, and a Taluk (Sub-divisional) Legal
Services Committee headed by a senior civil judge, deliver legal aid and organise Lok Adalats at the grassroots.
